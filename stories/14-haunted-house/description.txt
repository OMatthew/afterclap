Where did haunted houses come from? The usual story says they started in the 1930s, when Halloween pranks got out of hand and grown-ups looked for ways to keep kids busy. Old newspapers show the idea is older. A Chicago paper's 1912 party column already mentions the "haunted house" party. In 1915, the Presbyterian young people's class in Hurley, South Dakota, turned the manse into "A Haunted House": guests went "two at a time" up "the stairway that leads to death," and "at the top a ghost pounced upon them." A 1924 California paper's Halloween program had guests touch a dead man's "brains" (a sponge dampened with ice water) and his eyes (two peeled grapes) in the dark.

The 1930s pranks were real: tipped outhouses and soaped windows grew destructive, and towns such as Rhinelander, Wisconsin (1931) and Fargo, North Dakota (1934) threw big Halloween parties to keep kids busy. Later, haunted houses became fundraisers: in 1964 the Indianapolis Children's Museum Guild, a volunteer women's group, turned an old building into a haunted house and raised more than $13,000 its first year, and Jaycees chapters around the country ran charity haunted houses from the early 1970s.
