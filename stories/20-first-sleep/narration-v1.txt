In 1699, a young girl named Jane Rowth told an English court about the night her mother died.
She said the two of them had just woken from their "first sleep."

[[PAUSE 0.3]]

Three hundred years later, a historian named Roger Ekirch read that, and stopped. First sleep? Then what was the second?
So he went looking. He found it in diaries, court records, doctors' books and plays. About two thousand mentions, going back to ancient Greece.
For centuries, many Europeans slept in two parts. To bed soon after dark, awake for an hour or so around midnight, then back to sleep until dawn.
Doctors even said which side to lie on: the right side first, the left side second.
But as lamps got brighter and nights got later, the gap closed. By the early 1900s, the phrase was forgotten.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

Researchers still argue about how common it really was.
But when volunteers spent fourteen hours a night in the dark, for weeks, their sleep began to split in two.
And sleep doctors say that waking for a while in the night can be perfectly normal.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

Jane never explained what "first sleep" meant.
She didn't need to. Back then, everyone knew.
