## Cold listen (script clarity check)

**Chain as I heard it:** In 1699 a girl mentions "first sleep" in court. Centuries later a historian is puzzled by the phrase and finds it everywhere. People used to sleep in two parts with a waking hour between. Lamps ended that, and the phrase vanished. It's debated, but an experiment in darkness brought the pattern back. Jane didn't explain it because it was ordinary.

**Answer to "What was 'first sleep'?":** the first of two sleeps in one night. The script gets there, but never says "second sleep" as a thing; the listener has to assemble it from "slept in two parts".

**Hook payoffs:** "first sleep" pays off in "many Europeans slept in two parts". "Then what was the second?" is never answered in so many words. Jane pays off in the last two lines. "The night her mother died" is not part of the answer and is not returned to, which is acceptable as setting, but see strong suggestion 1.

**Pronouns:**
- "a historian read that": her testimony. Clear.
- "He found it in diaries": "it" could be the second sleep (the question just asked) or the phrase "first sleep". Points two ways. Blocking 1.
- "closed the gap": nothing has been called a gap. A listener has to work out that the gap is the waking hour. Blocking 3.
- "how common it was": the nearest noun is "the phrase", so this is heard as "how common the phrase was". Blocking 2.
- "She didn't need to": Jane. Clear.

**Lines a tired viewer needs twice:**
- "kept in the dark" is also the idiom for "not told". It resolves by "fourteen hours a night", but it stumbles first.
- "But volunteers ..." arrives with no anchor: whose volunteers, and when? One word ("in an experiment") would do, if there is room.
- "Centuries later" and "For centuries" sit two lines apart and chime.
- Three "But" turns in four lines ("But brighter lamps", "But volunteers"), which is fine for the story engine, though the second "But" suggests the experiment settles the historians' argument. It doesn't; it only shows the body can do it. Acceptable.

No repeated punchline, no over-explaining. The ending is a callback that adds a new idea (too ordinary to explain), so it does not just restate the hook.

## Blocking issues

1. **"He found it" points two ways, and the second sleep is never named.**
   Replace: `He found it in diaries, plays, and doctors' books. About two thousand times.`
   With: `He found both in diaries, plays, and doctors' books. About two thousand times.`
   And replace: `For centuries, many Europeans slept in two parts, with an hour or so awake around midnight.`
   With: `Many Europeans once slept twice a night, with an hour or so awake around midnight.`
   ("Both" answers "what was the second?" in the very next breath; "slept twice a night" is plainer than "in two parts"; dropping "For centuries" removes the chime with "Centuries later".)

2. **"how common it was" is heard as "how common the phrase was".**
   Replace: `Researchers still argue about how common it was.`
   With: `Researchers still argue about how common two sleeps were.`

3. **Cause stated flat, with an unexplained "gap".**
   Replace: `But brighter lamps and later nights closed the gap, and the phrase was forgotten.`
   With: `But as lamps got brighter, the two sleeps became one, and the phrase was forgotten.`
   ("As" is the hedge the research table asked for. If there is room, "as lamps got brighter and nights got later" is equally sourced.)

## Strong suggestions

1. **"died" to "was killed".** The Edgier topics rule says tell it straight. "Died" sounds like an illness, which leaves "why was a court involved?" hanging, and it softens a murder. "Was killed" is true (BBC: murdered that night), is not gory, and gives the first line its stakes. Don't say "murdered" in the title or cover. Put "the crime was never solved" in the description so the loop is closed somewhere.
   `In 1699, a girl named Jane told an English court about the night her mother was killed.`

2. **Bring the present-day line back, shorter.** Without it the Short is a pleasant curiosity; with it, anyone who wakes at 3 a.m. has a reason to care, which is what "Interesting first" asks for. The v2 wording "waking for a while" goes further than the doctors quoted, who talk about waking, not about an hour awake. Use:
   `And doctors say waking in the night can be normal.`
   Sourced: Drerup (Cleveland Clinic) in CNN, waking is "very normal based on our sleep architecture"; Wehr, "Waking up after a couple of hours may not be insomnia. It may be normal sleep." No advice, no "your ancestors did it".

3. **"kept in the dark" to "kept in darkness"**, to dodge the idiom:
   `But volunteers kept in darkness fourteen hours a night began sleeping in two halves.`

4. **Title.** "Why did a girl in 1699 say 'first sleep'?" has a number in it, and the packaging rule says titles carry no numbers. If that rule means years too, an option is "Why did a girl tell a court about her 'first sleep'?" (51 characters). If it only means episode numbers, the current title is fine. Lead's call.

5. **Drawings.** No red paint on the mother, the two men or the window scene. Land it on the document or the words "first sleep".

## Fine as is

- The opening: strangest true fact first, a named person, stakes inside ten seconds.
- 1699, Jane, "first sleep", two thousand, an hour or so, fourteen hours: all sourced. The number count (1699, two thousand, fourteen, plus "an hour or so") is within the rule.
- No volunteer head count, no "medieval", no "nobody slept through the night", no "1920s": every correction from the research is respected.
- The debate line is there and is fair to both sides.
- "Back then, everyone knew." Keep it. It is the best line and it is true.
- **Tone toward the victim:** respectful. No joke, no detail of the killing, and the script ends with Jane rather than using her and moving on. The only glib risk is the historian "stopping" at two words in a murder statement, but that is what happened and the line is told plainly.
- **Cover "Then what was second?"** Four words, adds a second puzzle, gives nothing away. Title and cover together make the question bigger; neither answers it. The cover repeats a line of narration, which is fine.
- On-screen question "What was 'first sleep'?" is paid off by the middle of the Short.
- No closing tease: allowed since 2026-10-22.
