## Cold listen (script clarity check)

**Chain as heard:** In 1699 a girl tells a court about the night her mother was killed and mentions waking from "first sleep". Centuries later a historian trips on the phrase, asks what the second was, and finds both all over old writing. People used to sleep twice a night with a waking hour in the middle, which they used to pray, talk or call on neighbours. Brighter lamps merged the two sleeps and the phrase died. An experiment in long darkness brought two-part sleep back, though how common it once was is still debated. Jane never explained the phrase because nobody then needed it explained.

**Answer to "What was 'first sleep'?":** the first of two sleeps in a night. Clear by the middle of the Short.

**Hook payoffs:**
- "first sleep" and "Then what was the second?": "He found both ..." and "Many Europeans once slept twice a night".
- Jane: "Jane never explained what 'first sleep' meant. She didn't need to."
- "the night her mother was killed": setting, not part of the answer, and not returned to. Acceptable (it explains why a court was involved); the description closes the loop with "never solved".

**Pronouns:**
- "a historian read that": Jane's testimony. Clear.
- "He found both": first sleep and second sleep, named in the two sentences before. Clear.
- "In that hour": the "hour or so awake" in the line before. Clear, and it hooks on well.
- "the phrase": "first sleep". Clear.
- "She didn't need to": Jane. Clear.

**Lines a tired listener might need twice (none blocking):**
- "once slept twice a night": "once ... twice" can be heard for a beat as two counts. "used to sleep twice a night" avoids it for one extra word.
- The debate line now follows the experiment with only a line break. It is heard correctly ("were" is past tense, and "two sleeps" is named), and it no longer lets the experiment sound like the answer to the historians' argument, which is better than v3. A short pause before it would help it land as its own thought.
- "debate how common" is fine by ear.

No repeated punchline, no over-explaining. One question pivot only ("First sleep? Then what was the second?"), which is the story's own question.

**The ending:** keeps. It returns to Jane, adds a new idea (too ordinary to explain) and does not restate the hook. "Everyone knew" is about the phrase, which even Boyce does not dispute. Respectful to the victim: no joke, no detail of the killing.

**Numbers:** 1699 (or the decade), two thousand, fourteen, plus "an hour or so". Within the rule.

## Title

**Pick (c): "What was 'first sleep'?"** (23 characters)
- (a) carries a number, which the title rule excludes, and it is the very number in doubt above.
- (b) puts "murder" in the title. The Edgier topics rule asks for factual titles with no shock words, and the murder is the setting, not the subject; a viewer who clicks for a murder story gets a sleep story.
- (c) is plain, has no number, does not give the answer away, and pairs with the cover as a two-step: "What was 'first sleep'?" then "Then what was second?" The cover makes the question bigger, as the packaging rule wants. Its weakness is that it repeats the on-screen question and is mild on its own; if the lead wants more pull without breaking a rule, "Why did a girl tell a court about her 'first sleep'?" (51 characters) is an option.

## Description

```
What was "first sleep"? On the night of 13 April 1699 (the date the BBC gives; CNN dates the document to 1697), in a village in the north of England, nine-year-old Jane Rowth and her mother woke from what Jane called their "first sleep". Two men came to the window, her mother went out with them, and she was later found dead. Jane said so in a deposition for an assize court. The crime was never solved.

Nearly 300 years later, the historian A. Roger Ekirch read her words at the Public Record Office in London and went looking for more. He has since counted about two thousand references to a first and second sleep, with an hour or so awake between them, when people prayed, talked or even visited neighbours. He dates the change to one long sleep to the 1800s, with artificial light as the main cause among several. In 1992, Thomas Wehr found that volunteers given 14 hours of darkness a night for four weeks began sleeping in two bouts.

Not everyone agrees on how common two sleeps were. Yetish and colleagues (2015) found that three societies living without electricity mostly sleep in one stretch, and the historian Niall Boyce (2023) argues the old texts are less clear than they look. Ekirch replied in 2024.
```
