## Cold listen (script clarity check)

**Chain as heard:** In the 1690s a girl tells a court about the night her mother was killed and mentions waking from "first sleep". Centuries later a historian trips on the phrase and finds first and second sleep all over old writing. People slept twice a night with a waking hour in between, used for prayer, talk or visits. Brighter lamps merged the two sleeps and the phrase died. Then: did the habit vanish too? Researchers argue how common it was. But an experiment in long darkness produced two-part sleep. Jane never explained the phrase because nobody needed it explained.

**Answer to "What was 'first sleep'?":** the first of two sleeps in a night. Still clear by the middle.

**Hook payoffs:** unchanged from v4 and still paid ("He found both", "slept twice a night", "Jane never explained").

**Pronouns:**
- "a historian read that", "He found both", "In that hour", "the phrase", "She didn't need to": clear, as in v4.
- "how common it was": the nearest noun is now "the habit" (from the question), so "it" is heard as the habit, not the phrase. That part works. It is weaker than v4's "how common two sleeps were", because the pronoun now leans on a question the listener is still trying to parse (below).

**Where a listener stumbles (the three new lines):**

1. *The question has just been answered.* "The two sleeps became one" already says the habit ended. Six words later: "But did the habit vanish too?" The "too" asks whether the habit went the way of the phrase, and the line before said it did. A cold listener thinks "you just told me it did". The question the writer means is a different one: can people still sleep that way?
2. *The next line answers a third question.* "Researchers still argue how common it was" is about the past, not about whether the habit vanished. Question, then a non-answer, then the answer: fact, teaser, new subject inside three sentences, which is what Written for the ear warns against.
3. *"But in one experiment" now rebuts the historians.* Debate, then "But", then the experiment: it is heard as "they argue, but the experiment shows it was real". Wehr's volunteers show what people can do in fourteen hours of dark today. They do not show how common two sleeps were in 1690. v4 was praised for not letting the experiment sound like the answer to the historians' argument; v5 brings that back.

Smaller points:
- "argue how common" drops "about". Clear beats clever: say "argue about how common".
- This is a second question beat after "First sleep? Then what was the second?". It is a re-hook, not an "And butter?" pivot, so it is allowed, but it has to be a question the next line answers.
- Two lines in a row now start with "But" ("But did...", "But in one...").

## Does the question overpromise?

Yes, as worded. "Did the habit vanish too?" promises a verdict on history or on people today in general. The experiment gives neither: it shows that a handful of volunteers, kept in the dark fourteen hours a night for weeks, drifted into two sleeps. That honestly answers "can people still sleep that way?" (yes, under those conditions), and nothing wider. Ask that question and the experiment pays it in full.

## Blocking issue

1. **The question, the debate line and the experiment are in an order that confuses and overclaims.** Keep the lead's idea (debate first, then a question, then the experiment) but ask the question the experiment answers, and put the pause between the debate and the question so the "But" turns away from the past instead of rebutting it.

   Replace:
   ```
   As lamps got brighter, the two sleeps became one, and the phrase was forgotten.
   But did the habit vanish too?

   [[PAUSE 0.4]]

   Researchers still argue how common it was.
   But in one experiment, volunteers kept in darkness fourteen hours a night began sleeping in two halves.
   ```
   With:
   ```
   As lamps got brighter, the two sleeps became one, and the phrase was forgotten.
   Researchers still argue about how common two sleeps were.

   [[PAUSE 0.4]]

   But can people still sleep that way?
   In one experiment, volunteers kept in darkness fourteen hours a night began sleeping in two halves.
   ```
   Net change: plus three words. "That way" points to "two sleeps" in the line before. The experiment now answers exactly what was asked, and makes no claim about the past.

   If the lead would rather drop the question: v4's order (experiment, short pause, "Researchers still debate how common two sleeps were.") already passed and is the fallback.

What was "first sleep"? On the night of 13 April 1699 (the date the BBC gives; CNN dates the document to 1697), in a village in the north of England, nine-year-old Jane Rowth and her mother woke from what Jane called their "first sleep". Two men came to the window, her mother went out with them, and she was later found dead. Jane said so in a deposition for an assize court. The crime was never solved.

Nearly 300 years later, the historian A. Roger Ekirch read her words at the Public Record Office in London and went looking for more. He has since counted about two thousand references to a first and second sleep, with an hour or so awake between them, when people prayed, talked or even visited neighbours. He dates the change to one long sleep to the 1800s, with artificial light as the main cause among several. In 1992, Thomas Wehr found that volunteers given 14 hours of darkness a night for four weeks began sleeping in two bouts.

Not everyone agrees on how common two sleeps were. Yetish and colleagues (2015) found that three societies living without electricity mostly sleep in one stretch, and the historian Niall Boyce (2023) argues the old texts are less clear than they look. Ekirch replied in 2024.
