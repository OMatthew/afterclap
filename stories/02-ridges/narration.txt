Why does a dime have ridges, but a nickel doesn't? Those ridges, and some bricked-up windows in England, trace back to one crime.

Silver coins used to be worth their silver. They were hammered out by hand, with uneven edges, so people snipped a sliver off each one and melted the clippings down.

You could hang for clipping. People clipped anyway.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

By 1695, England's silver coins were missing nearly half their silver.

So England called them in and struck new coins by machine, with ridged edges. Clip one of those, and anyone can see it.

For months, the old coins were taken back at full value, as if they were whole. That cost the government millions of pounds. To raise the money, Parliament taxed houses by their windows. You can count windows from the street.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Some owners bricked windows up, to pay less tax. A few of those blank windows are still there.

[[PAUSE 0.6]]

America's silver coins, dimes included, got ridges too. A nickel was never worth clipping, so it stayed smooth.

The silver left the dime in 1965. The ridges stayed.
