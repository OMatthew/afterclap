**Description**

Why do we carve pumpkins on Halloween? The usual answer is the Irish tale of Jack, who tricked the devil and was left to wander with a coal in a carved turnip. But the oldest carved lanterns we found were for scaring people. In a letter dated 1640, James Howell quoted a saying about people "afraid of a turnip cut like a death's head, with a candle in it." An English antiquarian, Jabez Allies (born 1787), remembered boys in Worcestershire hollowing out turnips, cutting eyes, nose and mouth, and setting them on hedges "to frighten unwary travellers in the night."

In America the lantern was a pumpkin. Jacob Abbott's children's book Rollo's Vacation (1838) explains how to make a jack-o'-lantern: dig out a pumpkin, cut a face, put in a candle and carry it out in the dark. Halloween isn't in the book. For decades pumpkin lanterns also turned up in a Thanksgiving poem, at election parades and, in Portsmouth, N.H., on Guy Fawkes night (Nov. 5). Historians say that after the Civil War Americans made Halloween a party night and folded in harvest decorations, pumpkins included; by 1885 Harper's Young People described American boys making jack-o'-lanterns for Halloween.

The oldest version of Jack's tale we could find, in the Dublin Penny Journal (1836), gives Jack a lantern but no coal, turnip or pumpkin. It is told to explain the ghostly light over marshes at night, the will-o'-the-wisp, which English speakers had called a jack-o'-lantern since the 1660s. How the name moved to carved lanterns isn't documented.
