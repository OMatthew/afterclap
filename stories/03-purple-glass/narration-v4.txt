Why is some old glass purple? It was made clear. Sunlight turned it purple. Newer glass doesn't, because of a machine that helped get kids out of bottle factories.

Iron in sand tints glass green. So glassmakers added a little manganese. Its faint pink cancels the green. They called it glassmaker's soap.

But sunlight slowly changes manganese. Over months and years, the glass turns lavender, then purple. A half-buried bottle turns purple only where the sun could reach it.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Why did the purple stop? World War One made manganese scarce. But mostly, it was a machine.

In 1903, Michael Owens got the first fully automatic bottle machine working. Machines drew their glass straight from huge open tanks, where manganese didn't work well. Glassmakers switched to selenium instead. Selenium glass ages to a faint straw color, not purple.

That's why purple glass usually means it was made before about 1920, while bottles were still blown by hand.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Owens had been one of those factory kids. He started at ten, carrying hot bottles. His machine took over the kids' jobs, and helped end child labor in bottle factories.
