Why is some old glass purple? It started out clear. Sunlight turned it purple. Newer bottles don't, mostly because of a machine that helped get kids out of bottle factories.

Iron in sand tints glass green. So glassmakers added a little manganese. Its faint pink cancels the green. They called it glassmaker's soap.

But sunlight slowly changes manganese. Over months and years, the glass can turn lavender, then purple. A half-buried bottle turns purple only where the sun could reach it.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Why did the purple stop? World War One made manganese scarce. But mostly, it was machines.

In 1903, Michael Owens got the first fully automatic bottle machine working. Machines drew their glass straight from huge open tanks, where manganese was hard to control. Most glassmakers switched to selenium. Selenium glass can age to a faint straw color, not purple.

That's why purple glass usually means it was made before about 1920, when most bottles were still blown by hand.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Owens had been one of those factory kids. He started at ten, shoveling coal and carrying hot bottles. His machine took over much of the kids' work, and helped end child labor in bottle factories.
