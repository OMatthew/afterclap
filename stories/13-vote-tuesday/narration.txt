Why do Americans vote on a Tuesday?
The usual answer is buggies and Sundays: farmers needed a day to ride to the polls without traveling on the Sabbath.
But in the 1845 debate we read, nobody says that.
[[PAUSE 0.4]]
Before 1845, each state picked its own day, anywhere in a thirty-four-day window before the electors met in December.
So lawmakers feared people could vote in one state, cross into the next, and vote again. Both parties had just accused each other of it.
[[PAUSE 0.4]]
So an Ohio congressman wrote a bill: one day, every state. The House passed it 187 to 1.
His draft said "the first Tuesday in November." But some years, that's November first, thirty-six days before the electors meet. Too early for the old rule.
So the law says "the Tuesday after the first Monday." That way the vote always lands twenty-nine days before.
[[PAUSE 0.4]]
And why Tuesday at all? One lawmaker who helped write it said the exact day was "of no importance."
[[PAUSE 0.5]]
Congress later used the same day for the House and the Senate. Which is why this year, it's Tuesday, November third.
