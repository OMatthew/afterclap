Why are maraschino cherries so red? Because the cherry isn't. The red is dye. And for nearly thirty years, the government called them imitations. Then the dye became the definition.

A brine preserves the cherries and bleaches them pale. Then they're dyed red and soaked in almond syrup.

The name comes from Croatia's coast, where dark marasca cherries were kept in maraschino liqueur.

You'll hear that Prohibition killed the liqueur, so America faked it. But the fake came first.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

By 1911, government chemists were opening jars of "maraschino cherries" and finding no liqueur at all. Just dyed cherries in almond syrup, some colored with insect dye. Shipments were seized, and sellers fined for false labels.

In 1912, the rule became: call them imitation, or don't call them maraschino.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

But to shoppers, a maraschino cherry was the red one. So in 1940, the government accepted that: a sweet, almond-flavored cherry, dyed red. The fake won the name.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Most jarred maraschinos use Red 40 today. But some cherries shipped in 1910 were dyed with erythrosine, now Red No. 3. After more than a century in our food, it's banned from January 2027.
