Why are maraschino cherries so red? Because the cherry isn't. The red is dye. And for nearly thirty years, the government called them fakes, then made the dye part of the definition.

The cherries sit in a brine that preserves them, and bleaches them pale. Then they're dyed red and soaked in almond-flavored syrup.

The name comes from Croatia's coast, where dark marasca cherries were kept in maraschino liqueur.

You'll often hear that Prohibition killed the liqueur, so America made a fake. But the fake came first.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

By 1911, government chemists were opening bottles of "maraschino cherries" and finding no maraschino at all. Just dyed cherries in almond syrup, some colored with crushed insects. Bottles were seized and makers fined.

In 1912, the government ruled they had to be labeled imitation.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

But shoppers already knew what a maraschino cherry was. So in 1940, the government gave in, and defined it as a cherry that's been dyed red. The fake won the name.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

One cherry tested back in 1910 was dyed with erythrosine, today called Red No. 3. After more than a century in our food, it's banned starting January 2027. Most maraschinos had already switched to Red 40.
