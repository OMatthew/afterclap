Why does the president pardon a turkey? The first time a president talked about it, he was dodging a question. On Nov. 23, 1987, at the National Turkey Federation's Rose Garden presentation, reporters asked Ronald Reagan whether he would pardon Oliver North and John Poindexter, his former aides caught up in the Iran-Contra affair. He wouldn't say. Instead he joked that he "would have pardoned" Charlie, the turkey (which was already headed for a pet farm). It's the first time on record that a president used the word for a turkey.

Presidents had been given live turkeys for decades: the National Turkey Federation has presented one since 1947, and the early birds were often meant for dinner (the Truman Library has no record of Truman pardoning one). Some presidents, like Kennedy in 1963, sent theirs back to a farm, but none called it a pardon. On Nov. 17, 1989, George H. W. Bush said his turkey had been "granted a Presidential pardon as of right now", and every president since has done the same.

It has no legal effect: the pardon power covers offenses against the United States, and the turkey hasn't committed one. And the birds are broad-breasted whites, bred to grow fast for meat, so many pardoned turkeys have lived only a year or two more (both of the 2012 birds died within a year, for example), though some, like Courage (2009), lived much longer.
