## B) Cold listen

### Script clarity check

1. **Chain, told back.** Turkey farmers have given presidents a live turkey since 1947, at first for eating. Some presidents let theirs live but nobody called that a pardon. In 1987 Reagan, asked whether he'd pardon two Iran-Contra figures, ducked and joked that he'd have pardoned the turkey. Two years later Bush actually declared the turkey pardoned, and every president since has copied him. It has no legal meaning, and the birds often don't live long anyway. Answer to "why does every president pardon one now?": because a joke became a line in the ceremony and nobody has dropped it. The chain holds. It answers "how did it start" more than "why does it go on"; that is acceptable for 50 seconds and honest (the "both sides get something" reading is interpretation and belongs in the description).

2. **Hook payoffs.**
   - "first time a president talked about pardoning a turkey" -> "But the turkey, he joked, he would have pardoned."
   - "dodging a question about a scandal" -> "would he pardon two aides from the Iran-Contra scandal? He wouldn't say."
   - "why does every president pardon one now?" -> "Two years later, George Bush said it ... Every president since has done the same."
   All three are the answer, not tangents. The second payoff is a little weak by ear: the listener has to join "He wouldn't say" and the joke to realise the joke was the dodge. "Instead" does that join (see proposed line).

3. **Pronouns.**
   - "pardon one" = a turkey. Clear.
   - "The early ones" = the early birds. Clear.
   - "spared theirs" / "sent his" = their / Kennedy's turkey. Clear.
   - "No president called it a pardon": "it" = sparing the bird. Clear enough.
   - "George Bush said it with no joke": "it" = ? (the word "pardon"; the joke; the promise). Loose; resolved by the clause after the colon, but only just.
   - "done the same" = pardoned a turkey. Clear.
   - **"It isn't a real pardon" / "And it was bred for the table": two "it"s in neighbouring lines pointing at different things** (the pardon, then the turkey). Rewrite the second with the noun.

4. **Heard the wrong way / "wait, what?" on one listen.**
   - **"But the turkey, he joked, he would have pardoned."** The line a tired listener needs twice. Object first, an interruption, then a "would have" with no "if": would have, if what? Did he pardon it or not? The true condition (the bird was already safe) is missing, so the conditional sounds like a slip.
   - **"said it with no joke: this turkey was pardoned."** "With no joke" is an unusual phrase and "was pardoned" in the past tense sounds as if someone had already pardoned it.
   - "it was bred for the table": the past tense "was" plus "it" makes this sound like one particular turkey. Also "bred for the table" is an idiom an English-as-second-language listener may miss; "bred to be eaten" is the plain version.
   - "Since 1947 ... Not until 1987": forty-seven and eighty-seven sit five lines apart and sound alike. Not confusing enough to change, since they are separated by the Kennedy beat, but Darren should land "nineteen eighty-seven" clearly. If the voice blurs them, "Since the 1940s" is an accurate fallback for the first.
   - "Iran-Contra": a younger listener won't know it, but "scandal" in the hook and in the same line carries it. Fine; named with context.

5. **Repeated punchlines / over-explaining.** None. "Pardon" recurs nine times but it is the subject. The close does not restate the hook.

### Pre-send check

1. **Insider words in passing:** "Iran-Contra" is named once with "scandal" beside it: fine. "bred for the table": swap for the plain phrase.
2. **Clever or indirect phrasing:** two spots, both flagged above. "But the turkey, he joked, he would have pardoned" is inverted for a punchline rhythm, and it costs clarity at the exact moment of the reveal. "Said it with no joke" is compressed. Say the plain thing in both.
3. **Hook promises not paid off:** none.
4. **The thing the story turns on is named plainly:** yes (the word "pardon", the 1987 question, the 1989 line).
5. **Every fact does work; ending doesn't restate the hook:** yes. Kennedy sets up "nobody called it a pardon"; the last two lines are a true coda.

Stakes inside 10 s (a president under pressure): yes. But/so turns: "But some presidents spared theirs", "Not until 1987", "He wouldn't say. But...", "Two years later". Mid-Short re-hook: "No president called it a pardon. Not until 1987." closes one question and opens the next: good, and better than v1's "That took a scandal". One question pivot (the reporters' question): within the limit. Line lengths vary.

## Proposed replacement lines

1. (minor, line 7)

   - Now: "But the turkey, he joked, he would have pardoned."
   - Proposed: "Instead, he joked that he would have pardoned the turkey, if it needed it."
   - Shorter option if the tail feels heavy: "Instead, he joked that he would have pardoned the turkey."
   - Why: plain word order; "Instead" makes the joke audibly the dodge the hook promised; "if it needed it" gives the "would have" its condition and is true (the bird was already headed for a pet farm).

3. (minor, line 10)
   - Now: "Two years later, George Bush said it with no joke: this turkey was pardoned."

   - Why: removes "with no joke" (his remarks were light-hearted too); the real contrast is would-have versus did. Present tense "is pardoned" matches "granted a Presidential pardon as of right now".

4. (**BLOCKING**, line 13)
   - Now: "And it was bred for the table, so many live only a year or two more."
   - Proposed: "And these birds are bred to be eaten, so many have lived only a year or two more."
   - Why: "have lived" is what the sources support and what v1 said; "these birds" fixes the double "it"; "bred to be eaten" is the plain form of the idiom. If the lead prefers to keep the sources' phrase: "And these birds are bred for the table, so many have lived only a year or two more."
