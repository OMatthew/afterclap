Why are oranges orange? One reason: cool nights. They help break down the green in the peel, and orange takes over. Where nights stay warm, a ripe orange can stay green. And green oranges ended up at the Supreme Court.

In Florida, early-season oranges often ripen green. From 1921, packers cleared the green with fumes from kerosene stoves, but the fruit came out pale yellow.

So in the 1930s, Florida began dyeing some of its oranges orange. Later, the boxes had to say "Color Added."

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In the 1950s, that dye failed new government tests and was struck off the approved list. Florida's growers sued. Their case: it's only a trace, on the peel.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In 1958, the Supreme Court ruled, unanimously: under the law, a dye found harmful can't be used at all. Not even a trace.

Then Congress allowed a different dye, as a stopgap, until it could write a new color law with safe limits. That law came in 1960, partly because of oranges. The FDA is now using it to end Red Number 3, the old maraschino-cherry dye.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

And that stopgap dye? It was never banned. The FDA certified its last batch in 2020. It seems nobody uses it now, and the FDA wants to retire it.
