Why are oranges orange? Cool nights. They break down the green in the peel, and orange takes over. Where nights stay warm, a ripe orange can stay green. And green oranges ended up at the Supreme Court.

In Florida, early-season oranges often ripen green. From 1921, packers used gas to clear the green, but the fruit came out pale yellow.

So from the 1930s, Florida dyed some of its oranges orange, marked "Color Added."

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In the 1950s, that dye failed the government's new tests, and was struck off the list. Florida's growers sued: it's only a trace, on the peel.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In 1958, the Supreme Court ruled, unanimously: under the law, a dye that isn't harmless can't be used at all. Not even a little.

So Congress allowed a stopgap dye, just until it could write a new color law, with safe limits. That law came in 1960, partly because of oranges. Under it, the FDA is now taking Red No. 3, the old cherry dye, out of our food.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

And that stopgap dye? It was never banned. Packers just stopped using it. The last batch was certified in 2020, and now the FDA wants it off the books.
