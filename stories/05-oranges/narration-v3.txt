Why are oranges orange? Cool nights. They break down the green in the peel, and orange takes over. Where nights stay warm, a ripe orange can stay green. And green oranges ended up at the Supreme Court.

In Florida, early-season oranges often ripen green. From 1921, packers used gas to clear the green, but the fruit came out pale yellow.

So from the 1930s, Florida dyed some of its oranges orange, marked "Color Added."

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In the 1950s, that dye failed the government's new tests, and was struck off the approved list. Florida's growers sued. Their case: it's only a trace, on the peel.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In 1958, the Supreme Court ruled, unanimously: under the law, a harmful dye can't be used at all. Not even a little.

So Congress allowed a different dye, as a stopgap, just until it could write a new color law, with safe limits. That law came in 1960, partly because of oranges. It's the law the FDA is using to end Red No. 3, the old cherry dye.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

And that stopgap dye? It was never banned. The last batch was approved in 2020. Packers just stopped using it, and now the FDA wants to retire it.
