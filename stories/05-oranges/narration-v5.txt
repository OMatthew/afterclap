Why are oranges orange? One reason: cool nights. They help break down the green in the peel, and orange takes over. Where nights stay warm, a ripe orange can stay green. And that green peel took Florida all the way to the Supreme Court.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

Florida's early oranges are often ripe but still green. In the 1920s, growers put them in rooms warmed by kerosene stoves, and the green faded. Not from the heat, scientists found. From the fumes. Even so, the oranges came out pale yellow, not orange.

So in the 1930s, Florida began dyeing some of its oranges orange.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In the 1950s, that dye failed new government tests, and it was banned. Florida's growers sued. It's just a trace on the peel, they said.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

In 1958, the Supreme Court ruled against them, unanimously. If a dye is found harmful, the law says, it can't be used at all. Not even a trace.

So Congress stepped in with a different dye, as a stopgap, until it could write a new color law with safe limits. That law came in 1960, partly because of oranges. Under that same law, the FDA is now banning Red No. 3, the old maraschino-cherry dye.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

And that stopgap dye? It was never banned. Growers just quietly stopped using it, and now the FDA wants to retire it.
