Why does Swiss cheese have fewer holes now? Because milking got too clean. And Swiss cheesemakers had to take their own government to court to get the holes back.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

The holes are gas. Bacteria added to the milk give off carbon dioxide as the cheese ripens. But a bubble needs a place to start.

In 2015, Swiss scientists found that place: tiny specks of hay. Hay stems are hollow, so each speck carries a little air. The gas gathers there, and a hole grows.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

Those specks used to fall into open milk pails in the barn. But from about 2005, Swiss farms switched to closed milking machines, and the milk came out cleaner than ever. Fewer specks, fewer holes.

[[PAUSE 0.4]]

So the makers of Emmentaler, the original Swiss cheese, asked to add a pinch of ground hay to the milk. Switzerland's agriculture office said no. It wasn't traditional.

In 2025, a Swiss federal court sided with the cheesemakers. The court said the hay powder isn't traditional, but the holes it brings back are.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

In America, the holes shrank on purpose. In 2001, the USDA cut the smallest hole in its top grade from about the size of a dime to about the size of a pea. The USDA said big holes tear in slicing machines.

[[PAUSE 0.5]]

So today, the smallest hole in real Emmentaler is about the biggest one in America's top-grade Swiss.
