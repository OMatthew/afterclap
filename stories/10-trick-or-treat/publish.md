**Description**

Why do we say "trick or treat"? Because it started as a threat. Before candy, Halloween night in North America meant pranks: gates carried off, wagons hauled onto barn roofs. The earliest known printed "tricks or treats" is in the Sault Daily Star (Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario), Nov. 1, 1917: "'Tricks or treats' you could hear the gangs call out, and if the householder passed out the 'coin' for the 'treats' his establishment would be immune from attack until another gang came along that knew not of or had no part in the agreement." Through the 1930s, US papers wrote it up like a shakedown.

By the 1970s the fear had flipped: parents were warned that strangers hid poison and razor blades in Halloween treats. The sociologist Joel Best (University of Delaware) has tracked these reports back to 1958 and has not found a substantiated report of a child killed or seriously injured by a contaminated treat picked up while trick-or-treating. The best-known death, in 1974 in Texas, was a boy poisoned by his own father. Even so, homemade treats fell out of favor, and candy sealed in a factory wrapper became the one that counted as safe.
