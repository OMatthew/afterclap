**Is it fair to give only the wax-paper worry, with "One worry"?** Yes.
- "One worry" says out loud that there were others, so the listener is not told this was the whole case. That is the right size of hedge for a claim about motive; a bare "The reason:" would have been too strong.
- Wax paper is the reason all four sources give (Smithsonian, Mental Floss, Atlas Obscura, Wikipedia), the one that officials explained in their own words, and the one the lifting statement answers directly. Flour price appears in three of the four; steel in one (plus TIME 1943). Choosing wax paper is choosing the best-attested reason, not the most convenient.
- It is also the reason the script pays off ("enough wax paper after all"), so plant and payoff match.
- Sources do not rank the reasons. Smithsonian leads with flour cost and gives wax paper as "also"; Mental Floss the same. So the script must not be edited later into "the main worry" or "the reason". "One worry" is the correct ceiling.

## 2. Cold-listener clarity check (v3)

**Chain, told back after one listen.** In 1943 the U.S. made selling sliced bread illegal. It was new and popular, but the country was at war and officials thought it was wasteful: a sliced loaf needs thicker wax paper so it doesn't go stale, and they feared wax paper would run short. So bakeries stopped slicing. Families objected, went back to cutting bread by hand, and one mother wrote to the Times about household "morale and saneness". Under two months later the ban ended; officials said it saved less than hoped and there was enough wax paper anyway. The Times headline: housewives' thumbs safe again. Answer: to save wax paper in wartime, and it wasn't worth it.

**Hook payoff.** "Illegal to sell sliced bread" is paid off by "to officials, it looked wasteful" and "One worry: ... wax paper might run short", and closed by "the ban was dropped". Every hook item is part of the answer.

**Pronouns.**
- "it looked wasteful": sliced bread. Clear.
- "or it dries out": the loaf. Clear.
- "it had saved less": the ban, named in the sentence before. Clear.
- "they'd hoped": officials, same sentence. Clear.
- "put it in a headline": the end of the ban. Clear.
- v2's "so they wanted" is gone with the flour line; no new pronoun was added.

**One-listen traps.**
- **The reason line is three clauses on one breath (not blocking).** "...or it dries out, and wax paper might run short" can be heard for a beat as a second thing that happens if the wrapping is too thin, since "and" follows straight after "or it dries out". The meaning recovers by the end of the line, and the payoff line settles it, so this passes; but it is the one place a tired listener might want to hear twice. See optional edit 1.
- **"Saved less"**: saved what is not said. With only wax paper planted it will be heard as paper, and the next clause is about paper, so it lands. Officials' "savings" was general, so the vagueness is also the accurate choice.
- **"Saneness"**: unchanged from v2. Real quote, unusual word, close to "sameness" in Darren's accent. Show the six words on screen as he says them.
- **"Only fifteen years old"**: for a beat it can sound like a person. The loaf drawing carries it.
- **"Thumbs"**: still set up by "sawing loaves by hand". Keep that plant.

**Repeated punchlines, over-explaining.** None. Wax paper is said three times (reason, reason, payoff); that is plant and payoff, not repetition.

**Insider words, question pivots.** None. "One worry:" is a noun-plus-colon turn of the kind the rules allow, and it is the only one.

**But / so.** Illegal; but it was new; but war, so officials saw waste; so bakeries stopped; but families objected; so (after that) the ban was dropped. With the mayor cut there is no "and then" beat left.

**Re-hook, head fake, ending.** "But families didn't take it quietly." still closes the ban and opens the fight. "Enough wax paper after all" is now a cleaner "of course" because wax paper is the only reason planted. The headline is a new, true button.
