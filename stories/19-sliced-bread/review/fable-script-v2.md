## 1. Fact check

**Central link, two independent sources.** Ban on January 18, 1943 by Food Distribution Order 1, for wax paper and bread price: Smithsonian (2025) and Mental Floss (Reilly, 2019, working from the Times archive), plus Atlas Obscura. Lifting on March 8, 1943 with Wickard's two reasons: Smithsonian and Atlas Obscura, plus History Facts. Passes the rule.

**"After the outcry": fair.** It states order in time, not cause, and the next sentence gives the officials' own reasons, which don't mention the complaints. Smithsonian and Atlas Obscura both stop short of saying the outcry caused the reversal; Wikipedia says the outcry is "generally credited". The script claims less than that. Keep the two sentences together: if the officials' reasons were ever cut, "after the outcry" alone would read as cause.

**Overstatement: none found.** Two things the script leaves out, both fine to leave out:
- Saving steel was also given as a reason at the time (TIME 1943: 100 tons of slicing-machine steel a year; Atlas Obscura). The script doesn't say wax paper and price were the only reasons, so nothing is false. Keep the description's wording open ("reasons officials gave included").
- The agencies later blamed each other for the idea (Mental Floss). Not needed.

**What I could not check.** The Times pages themselves: nytimes.com is blocked from this environment, and one Times address and my first Wikipedia request timed out on permission. The letter wording, headline wording and dates are therefore confirmed through several secondary sources that quote and link the Times, not from the page scans. The secondary sources agree with each other on every point except the March 6 / March 9 headline date.

## 2. Cold-listener clarity check

**Chain, told back after one listen.** In 1943 the U.S. banned the sale of sliced bread. It was wartime, and officials thought slicing was wasteful: a sliced loaf needs heavier wax paper so it doesn't dry out, and flour had gone up, so they wanted bread to stay cheap. Bakeries stopped slicing. Families hated it and cut bread by hand; one mother wrote to the Times, and New York's mayor complained. Under two months later the ban ended; officials said it saved less than hoped and there was plenty of wax paper. The Times headline joked that thumbs were safe. Answer to the question: to save wax paper and hold down the price of bread, and it didn't work.

**Hook payoff.** "Illegal to sell sliced bread" is paid off by "to officials, it looked wasteful" plus the two reasons (lines 5 and 6), and closed by "the ban was dropped". Nothing in the hook is a side tangent.

**Pronouns.**
- Line 5, "it looked wasteful": sliced bread. Clear.
- Line 6, "or it dries out": the loaf. Clear.
- Line 6, "so they wanted": officials, named one sentence back. Clear enough.
- Line 11, "it hadn't saved as much": the ban. Clear. Saved what is left open (paper, money), which is fine because "enough wax paper" follows at once.
- Line 12, "put it in a headline": the end of the ban. Clear.

**Lines heard the wrong way, or "wait, what?".**
- **Line 6, second half (blocking).** "Flour had just gotten pricier, so they wanted to keep bread cheap" gives the motive but never says how a slicing ban keeps bread cheap. The wax-paper reason comes with its "because"; the price reason doesn't, and it is half the answer to the title. The listener has to work out that not slicing saves bakers money. "Written for the ear" says never leave the viewer to infer the point. See edit 1.
- **Line 9, "saneness".** An unusual word, heard once in Darren's accent, and close to "sameness". It is the real quote, so keep it, but show the six words on screen as he says them.
- **Line 5, "only fifteen years old".** For a beat it can sound like a person. Minor; the drawing of a loaf will carry it. No change needed.
- **Line 12, "Thumbs".** Lands because line 9 planted "sawing loaves by hand". Keep that plant.

**Repeated punchlines, over-explaining.** None. "So bakeries had to stop slicing" restates line 1 from the bakery side; it works as the "so" beat but can be shorter (edit 2).

**Insider words.** None. "Wax paper" is everyday, and "officials" is plain. No question pivots.

**But / so test.** Illegal; but it was new and loved; but war, so officials saw waste; so bakeries stopped; but families objected; ("and then" the mayor complained); so, or at least after that, the ban was dropped. The mayor line is the only "and then" beat: it adds a name, not a turn.

## 3. Title, cover, stakes, re-hook

- **Title "Why did America ban sliced bread?"**: answered in lines 5 and 6 (waste in wartime: wax paper and bread price). True without a hedge.
- **On-screen "Why ban sliced bread?"**: fine for 1.5 s.
- **Cover "Illegal. In 1943."**: true, and it matches the first line. It doesn't say what was illegal, so the drawing has to be an unmistakable sliced loaf.
- **Stakes in the first 10 s**: present but light. "America was at war" and officials who wanted to save are in by about 8 s. The people who stood to lose (families) arrive at line 8, about 22 s in. Acceptable; no invented drama needed.
- **Mid re-hook**: "But families didn't take it quietly." closes the ban and opens the fight in one line, at roughly 22 to 25 s. Good. The mayor line that follows is where attention could sag.
- **Head fake fair in hindsight**: yes. "Enough wax paper after all" pays off the wax paper planted in line 6.
- **Ending**: the headline is a new, true button, not a restated hook.

## 4. Suggested line edits (three)

1. **Line 6, say how the ban keeps bread cheap (blocking).**
   Now: "And flour had just gotten pricier, so they wanted to keep bread cheap."
   To: "And flour had just gotten pricier. Dropping the slicing was meant to keep bread cheap."
   Reason: gives the price reason its "because" in plain words, so both halves of the answer are said out loud. Still accurate (the ban was meant to offset the flour price rise). Plus 2 words.

2. **Lines 7 and 10, tighten the "so" beat and cut the mayor.**
   Now: "So bakeries had to stop slicing." ... "New York's mayor complained too."
   To: "So bakeries stopped slicing." and cut the mayor line.
   Reason: the mayor line is the one "and then" beat, it names no reason, and sources disagree on what exactly he objected to. Cutting it lets the mother's quote run straight into the reversal. Minus 7 words. (If the lead wants the mayor kept, keep it as "complained"; don't add his reasons.)

3. **Line 11, trim the officials' reasons.**
   Now: "Officials said it hadn't saved as much as they'd hoped, and there was enough wax paper after all."
   To: "Officials said it had saved less than they'd hoped, and there was enough wax paper after all."
   Reason: same meaning, easier to hear than the double negative shape "hadn't saved as much as". Minus 2 words. Keep "after the outcry" in the sentence before.

Word count after all three: about 133. I see no further cut that is safe within three edits: don't cut "People went back to sawing loaves by hand", because the closing headline depends on it. At Darren's pace 133 words should still land inside 55 s.

**Also fix outside the script:** in `research/19-sliced-bread.md`, the headline date should be March 9, 1943 (not March 6), and the letter row should carry the "morale and saneness" sentence, since that is the quote the script uses.
