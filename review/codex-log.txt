# Channel rules (Afterclap)

Owner: Claude and its agent team. Matthew gives a human eye/ear at the gates below.
Started 2026-10-05.

## What the channel is
Vertical Shorts (60–75 s). Each one answers a real question about an everyday thing with a TRUE chain of cause and effect that lands somewhere nobody expects. Wonder plus dry wit. No morals.

## The groove check (Matthew's rule)
At every key decision (story, angle, hook, title, visual, music, name), ask: **"Is this what most AI would choose?"** If yes, choose something less obvious.
- Keep a running groove list (see `research/STORY_BANK.md`). Stories on it are out.
- The groove is wider than famous stories. Most everyday "why" questions already have near-zero-view AI Shorts. **Our edge is the far end of the chain**, so the script leads toward the surprising link fast.
- Cross-check: if ChatGPT and Claude both give the same story when asked cold, it's taken.

## The thread (Matthew's rule, 2026-10-06)
Every Short's last line teases the next Short, so the Shorts form one continuous thread. **Loosened (Matthew, 2026-10-22):** the thread is a planning aid, not a hard rule. Aim for it, but seasonal specials (e.g. a Halloween Short), a Short with no tease, a gap before a tease is paid off, or starting a fresh chain are all fine when timing or the story calls for it. A tease must still be paid off eventually.
- **Verify before teasing.** The next story is chosen and deep-verified (central link with two sources) before the current Short's voice is recorded. Short 02 teased purple glass before the research, and the research then found the popular explanation was a myth. The topic survived, but it might not have.
- **Write backward.** Pick the next story first, then plant its object on screen earlier in the current Short, so the tease feels earned (the window tax put glass on screen before the purple-glass tease).
- **Real bridges only.** The link is a shared thing (an object, material, place or person), never a pun. Best when it's also a shared idea (02 to 03: hand-made giving way to machines). Show the shared thing in the last scene, alongside the next story's object (02 ends on a bottle on a windowsill).
- **Each Short stands alone.** The thread lives only in the last line ("And why ...? That's another story."). Never require an earlier video.
- **Two ahead.** Keep two verified candidates for the next link, so a dud or a failed check doesn't break the thread.
- **Make it clickable.** When a Short goes live, set the previous Short's related video to it, and add it to the in-order playlist.
- **Track it** in `research/THREAD.md`.
- **Deliberate exceptions** (same look, feel and purpose): entry-point videos in a slightly different format that welcome new viewers into the thread, and special standalone Shorts. They sit outside the numbered thread.

## Accuracy
- Every claim in a script has a source in that video's fact table. Two independent reliable sources for the central link.
- Hedge causality: "part of the reason," "lined up with." Never "all because."
- If the popular version is a myth, say so or skip it.
- **True, not timid (Matthew, 2026-10-09).** Pick the most interesting phrasing that is still true. Hedge claims of cause ("partly because"), not every noun: "Why was margarine once dyed pink?" beats "Why did some states want margarine dyed pink?", and "Why is old glass purple?" doesn't claim all old glass is. A title or line only needs a hedge when a fair viewer would hear it as false without one.
- Sources go in every description.

## Openness about AI (Matthew approved 2026-10-05)
About section: short, true stories, fact-checked, sources in every description. AI disclosure stays quiet: platform AI labels, one footer line in the YouTube description ("Made with the help of AI tools.") and the bios ("Made with the help of AI tools", Matthew's wording, 2026-10-18); never in captions, covers or on screen. Keep visuals stylized; never a photoreal face of a real person (this also keeps us outside YouTube's AI-label requirement).

## Script clarity check (every draft and every edit)
Added 2026-10-06 after the dime Short's v3 edit cut the line that explained the goblin, and nobody re-checked it.
A fresh reviewer that hasn't seen the research (a separate, cold model call) reads the final script once, as a listener, and must:
1. Explain the whole chain back, ending with the answer to the opening question.
2. For each thing the hook promises, quote the line that pays it off. Every hook item should be part of the answer, not a side tangent.
3. Name what every pronoun points to (it, that, they, he), especially right after a reveal. Rewrite any that could point two ways.

## Written for the ear (Matthew, 2026-10-09)
Viewers hear the script once, at Darren's pace, with an accent. After Short 05's first voice:
- Each new point hooks onto the last one ("Florida's early oranges are often ripe but still green" after "where nights stay warm"), with a pause before a jump. Don't go from a fact to a teaser to a new place inside two sentences.
- No trade jargon ("packers"); use the word a viewer would use ("growers").
- If a word fights what the viewer expects ("fumes" when they expect heat), say why in the line: "Not from the heat, scientists found. From the fumes."
- Read it aloud for accidental rhymes and chimes ("Their case: it's only a trace").
- **Clear beats clever (Matthew, 2026-10-09).** Don't drop words to sound tight. Say the noun ("another reason: dye", not "another: dye"), say what a thing is for or where it goes ("still used on oranges today", "off the approved list"), and swap insider shorthand for plain words ("the first oranges of the fall", not "early oranges"). Before voicing, a plain-language pass lists every spot a general listener, or one with English as a second language, would need to hear twice.
- **Go easy on the question pivot (Matthew, 2026-10-10).** "And butter? Under a 1938 law..." (or "And butter: ...", which Darren reads as a question anyway) is fine once in a while, but more than once a video it smells like a bot, and 01, 05 and 06 all used it. At most once per video, and prefer a plain turn: "As for butter, ...". The opening question and the closing tease don't count.
- **Generally accurate, interesting and clear beats precise, clever and terse (Matthew, 2026-10-10).** The listener hears each line once, at speed, while we hop between ideas. No insider terms dropped in passing ("packers" in 05, "its top grade" in 07), and no clever compressions that make a rule or a thing "ask for" something. If a detail only makes a line more exact (which grade, which office, which section), cut it unless leaving it out would make a fair listener hear something false. Test each line read aloud once: would a tired viewer follow it without rewinding? Before (07): "The smallest hole its top grade asks for went from about a dime to about a pea." After: "In 2001, the USDA changed its rules. Good Swiss cheese used to need holes about the size of a dime. Now they can be as small as a pea." Reviewers (cold listens, fact checks, Astra) get this rule too, so they stop pushing lines toward legal precision. And every fact has to do work in the chain: if a line sets something up ("it has an official rulebook"), say what it means for the story in the same breath ("its rules say it has to have holes"), or cut it. Never leave the viewer to infer the point.
- **Pre-send script check (Matthew, 2026-10-14, after 08 v3 slipped).** Before any script goes to Matthew or to voice, the lead runs this list line by line, and every reviewer prompt includes it: (1) Insider words are allowed, but not in passing. If more than about half of viewers probably won't know a word, and it matters to following the story, explain it in a few words (or let the drawing show it, e.g. the wheel labeled while we say "Emmentaler, the original Swiss cheese"). If it adds nothing, use the everyday word. (2) Clever or indirect phrasing is judged case by case. For each one ask: why does this need to be said indirectly? Is it actually funny or ironic? Does it foreshadow or create curiosity that the video pays off? If the only reason is that it sounds clever, say the plain thing. (3) Does the hook promise something the video doesn't pay off? A hook detail that sounds like the topic (holes) must be the topic. (4) If the story turns on a specific thing (a salt, a dye), name it once, in plain words. (5) Every fact does work; nothing restates the hook as the ending.
- **Name the thing, with context (Matthew, 2026-10-20).** A real name or term (Ragamuffin Day, sodium phosphate) is not the problem; dropping it in cold is. When a story has a name people would search or remember, keep it and build up to it so it makes sense where it lands ("They looked like little ragamuffins, so people called it Ragamuffin Day"). Cutting the name to avoid explaining it is the wrong fix.
- Matthew reviews the voice before drawings start; cold reads are done as a listener, not a reader.

## Story engine (from Kallaway's storytelling videos, 2026-10-19)
Matthew pointed us to Kallaway (stakes, question, head fake, re-hook; "but/therefore"; rhythm). Most of it we already do: the question opens every Short, the surprising cause is the head fake, the closing tease is the re-hook. What we add, as checks on every script:
- **Stakes in the first ~10 s.** Not just a question: someone who wanted something or stood to lose something (the gangs vs the householder; the city vs the kids). One plain clause is enough. No invented drama.
- **But / so between beats, never "and then".** Read the beats in order and join them with "but" or "so" (South Park's rule). A beat that only joins with "and then" is a list item: cut it, or find the real turn and say it ("But the kids didn't stop.").
- **A mid-Short re-hook.** Around the middle (25-40 s), where attention drops, close one small question and open the next in the same line ("The men gave up their parades. The kids didn't."). Never leave a beat where the viewer feels finished before the end.
- **The head fake must be "of course" in hindsight.** The surprise is planted earlier in the script, so it feels fair, not random. (Already our accuracy rule: the twist is the true cause.)
- **Rhythm.** Put each sentence on its own line; if the line lengths all look the same, vary them: one short line after a long one lands the turn.
- **Show while you tell.** The first frame shows the thing the question is about, before the first word lands.
We don't take the casino framing literally: no fake cliffhangers, no withheld answers, no manufactured outrage. Calm and honest beats compulsive.

## Look
- Black-and-white line art on warm paper, lots of empty space.
- One accent color appears as **real paint**. It splashes onto the thing that matters, then lifts off and jumps to the next thing as the scene changes. The paint carries the chain, so the narration never needs to say "chain."
- No on-screen text except the cover frame, captions, the opening question (hook test) and hand-written numbers (`scene.letters`: a year, a price, a count, written on in our own pen strokes as it is spoken; at most one or two per Short, in scenes of 4 s or more) (2026-11-10).
- The paint is red lead #D9431E, the channel's signature.

Red paint on something we call purple reads as a contradiction. In the video it turns the color where it sits ("the glass turns purple" while it fills the bottle), holds it, then turns back to red before or while it jumps on. On the cover, the splash is the color the title or cover names (`cover.color` in layout.json; Short 03's cover went purple on 2026-10-08); the brand dot stays red. When the paint only marks a thing (the iron specks on "green"), it stays red. Stains, footprints and specks keep the color they had. Red stays the signature everywhere else.       const asp = tr ? tr.size[0] / tr.size[1] : 1;
      const w = Math.round(Math.min(bw, bh * asp));
      return { id: a.id, x: cx, y: cy, w };
    });
    const paint = sc.paint.map((p, i) => ({
      word: p.word, t: p.t,
      art: sc.art[Math.min(i, sc.art.length - 1)].id,
      at: [0.5, 0.5],
    }));
    return { id: sc.id, art, paint };
  });
  return {
    style: { ink: 6.4, inner: 0.68, minLen: 16, boil: 0.35, boilFps: 4, reel: { on: true } },
    cover: { words: ['Why?'], art: shots.scenes[0].art[0].id, at: [0.5, 0.5] },
    scenes,
  };
}

// merge: keep everything already in `cur`, add missing scenes / art / paint entries
function merge(def, cur) {
  if (!cur) return def;
  const out = JSON.parse(JSON.stringify(cur));
  out.style = Object.assign({}, def.style, cur.style || {});
  out.cover = cur.cover || def.cover;
  out.scenes = def.scenes.map(ds => {
    const cs = (cur.scenes || []).find(s => s.id === ds.id);
    if (!cs) return ds;
    const art = ds.art.map(da => (cs.art || []).find(a => a.id === da.id) || da);
    const paint = ds.paint.map((dp, i) => {
      const cp = (cs.paint || []).find(p => p.word === dp.word && Math.abs(p.t - dp.t) < 0.4);
      return cp ? Object.assign({}, dp, cp, { t: dp.t, word: dp.word }) : dp;
    });
    return Object.assign({}, cs, { id: ds.id, art, paint });
  });
  return out;
}

function loadLayout(story, shots, traced, { write = true } = {}) {
  const file = path.join(story, 'layout.json');
  const cur = fs.existsSync(file) ? JSON.parse(fs.readFileSync(file, 'utf8')) : null;
  const lay = merge(defaultLayout(shots, traced), cur);
  if (write && JSON.stringify(lay) !== JSON.stringify(cur)) fs.writeFileSync(file, JSON.stringify(lay, null, 1) + '\n');
  return lay;
}

  // typographic double quotes (Fable, Short 04: a straight " read as a stray mark)

  }).map(f => f.replace(/\.png$/i, ''));

function defaultLayout(shots, traced) {
  const scenes = shots.scenes.map(sc => {
    const pat = PATTERNS[Math.min(4, sc.art.length)] || PATTERNS[4];

      const w = Math.round(Math.min(bw, bh * asp));
      return { id: a.id, x: cx, y: cy, w };
    });
    const paint = sc.paint.map((p, i) => ({
      word: p.word, t: p.t,
      art: sc.art[Math.min(i, sc.art.length - 1)].id,

  });

// merge: keep everything already in `cur`, add missing scenes / art / paint entries
function merge(def, cur) {
  if (!cur) return def;
  const out = JSON.parse(JSON.stringify(cur));
  out.style = Object.assign({}, def.style, cur.style || {});
  out.cover = cur.cover || def.cover;
  out.scenes = def.scenes.map(ds => {
    const cs = (cur.scenes || []).find(s => s.id === ds.id);
    if (!cs) return ds;
    const art = ds.art.map(da => (cs.art || []).find(a => a.id === da.id) || da);
    const paint = ds.paint.map((dp, i) => {
      const cp = (cs.paint || []).find(p => p.word === dp.word && Math.abs(p.t - dp.t) < 0.4);
      return cp ? Object.assign({}, dp, cp, { t: dp.t, word: dp.word }) : dp;
    });
    return Object.assign({}, cs, { id: ds.id, art, paint });
  });
  return out;
}

function loadLayout(story, shots, traced, { write = true } = {}) {
  const file = path.join(story, 'layout.json');

  const lay = merge(defaultLayout(shots, traced), cur);
  if (write && JSON.stringify(lay) !== JSON.stringify(cur)) fs.writeFileSync(file, JSON.stringify(lay, null, 1) + '\n');

}

//   { "name": "01-08", "stories": ["01-dime", "02-ridges", ...], "gap": 0.6,

function relayout(ls, traced) {
  const boxes = ls.art.map(a => {
    const tr = traced[a.id], h = tr.size[1] * a.w / tr.size[0];

  });
  // groups: union of boxes overlapping by more than 12% of the smaller one
  const par = boxes.map((_, i) => i), find = i => par[i] === i ? i : (par[i] = find(par[i]));

    const A = boxes[i], B = boxes[j];
    const ix = Math.max(0, Math.min(A.x1, B.x1) - Math.max(A.x0, B.x0)), iy = Math.max(0, Math.min(A.y1, B.y1) - Math.max(A.y0, B.y0));
    if (ix * iy > 0.12 * Math.min(A.w * A.h, B.w * B.h)) par[find(i)] = find(j);
  }
  const groups = {};
  boxes.forEach((b, i) => (groups[find(i)] = groups[find(i)] || []).push(b));

    return { bs, x0, x1, y0, y1, w: x1 - x0, h: y1 - y0, cy: (y0 + y1) / 2, order: Math.min(...bs.map(b => firstPaint(b.id))) };
  }).sort((a, b) => a.order - b.order || a.cy - b.cy);

// style.loop: in the last moments the paint and its stains fade into the paper, so the last frame matches the
// clean first frame when Shorts loops (Matthew, 2026-10-30). style.loopFade = [seconds before the end it starts, ends]

}

import json, math, os

crop = {}
def C(aid):
    if aid not in crop:
        crop[aid] = json.load(open(os.path.join(HERE, 'art', 'traced', aid + '.json')))['crop']
    return crop[aid]
def frac(aid, x, y):
    x0, y0, w, h = C(aid)
    return [round((x - x0) / w, 4), round((y - y0) / h, 4)]
def word_n(w, n, after):
    hits = [x['s'] for x in words if x['w'].lower().strip(".,?:'\"") == w.lower() and x['s'] >= after - 1e-6]
    return hits[n - 1]

def word_t(w, after):
    return next(x['s'] for x in words if x['w'].lower().strip(".,?") == w and x['s'] >= after)

# where each drawing sits in its scene: scene id -> [(art id, x, y, w)], scene-local 1080x1920 px
def fit(aid, wmax=960, x=540, top=200, bottom=1330):
    """Largest placement of a drawing inside the box above the caption band (captions at y ~1434)."""
    _, _, cw, ch = C(aid)
    w = min(wmax, (bottom - top) * cw / ch)
    return (aid, x, round((top + bottom) / 2), round(w))

    1: [fit('plane-cat', 1000, top=620)],    # below the on-screen question (style.introTitle)

        e = {'word': p['word'], 't': p['t'], 'art': aid, 'at': frac(aid, *tgt), 'R': R}
        x0, y0, cw, ch = C(aid)
        if 'drain' in ex and 'drain' not in p:

        if 'poly' in ex:

        if 'ellipse' in ex:   # (cx, cy, a, b[, rotation in degrees])
            cx, cy, a, b, *rot = ex['ellipse']
            r = math.radians(rot[0] if rot else 0)
            pts = [(cx + a * math.cos(u) * math.cos(r) - b * math.sin(u) * math.sin(r),
                    cy + a * math.cos(u) * math.sin(r) + b * math.sin(u) * math.cos(r))
                   for u in (2 * math.pi * k / 48 for k in range(48))]
            e['fill'] = {'poly': [frac(aid, *q) for q in pts], 'drain': p['drain'], 'dripX': round(ex.get('dripX', 0) / cw, 4)}
        if 'slide_to' in ex:

        if 'soak' in p:

}- **Generally accurate, interesting and clear beats precise, clever and terse (Matthew, 2026-10-10).** The listener hears each line once, at speed, while we hop between ideas. No insider terms dropped in passing ("packers" in 05, "its top grade" in 07), and no clever compressions that make a rule or a thing "ask for" something. If a detail only makes a line more exact (which grade, which office, which section), cut it unless leaving it out would make a fair listener hear something false. Test each line read aloud once: would a tired viewer follow it without rewinding? Before (07): "The smallest hole its top grade asks for went from about a dime to about a pea." After: "In 2001, the USDA changed its rules. Good Swiss cheese used to need holes about the size of a dime. Now they can be as small as a pea." Reviewers (cold listens, fact checks, Astra) get this rule too, so they stop pushing lines toward legal precision. And every fact has to do work in the chain: if a line sets something up ("it has an official rulebook"), say what it means for the story in the same breath ("its rules say it has to have holes"), or cut it. Never leave the viewer to infer the point.
- **Pre-send script check (Matthew, 2026-10-14, after 08 v3 slipped).** Before any script goes to Matthew or to voice, the lead runs this list line by line, and every reviewer prompt includes it: (1) Insider words are allowed, but not in passing. If more than about half of viewers probably won't know a word, and it matters to following the story, explain it in a few words (or let the drawing show it, e.g. the wheel labeled while we say "Emmentaler, the original Swiss cheese"). If it adds nothing, use the everyday word. (2) Clever or indirect phrasing is judged case by case. For each one ask: why does this need to be said indirectly? Is it actually funny or ironic? Does it foreshadow or create curiosity that the video pays off? If the only reason is that it sounds clever, say the plain thing. (3) Does the hook promise something the video doesn't pay off? A hook detail that sounds like the topic (holes) must be the topic. (4) If the story turns on a specific thing (a salt, a dye), name it once, in plain words. (5) Every fact does work; nothing restates the hook as the ending.
- **Name the thing, with context (Matthew, 2026-10-20).** A real name or term (Ragamuffin Day, sodium phosphate) is not the problem; dropping it in cold is. When a story has a name people would search or remember, keep it and build up to it so it makes sense where it lands ("They looked like little ragamuffins, so people called it Ragamuffin Day"). Cutting the name to avoid explaining it is the wrong fix.
- Matthew reviews the voice before drawings start; cold reads are done as a listener, not a reader.

## Story engine (from Kallaway's storytelling videos, 2026-10-19)
Matthew pointed us to Kallaway (stakes, question, head fake, re-hook; "but/therefore"; rhythm). Most of it we already do: the question opens every Short, the surprising cause is the head fake, the closing tease is the re-hook. What we add, as checks on every script:
- **Stakes in the first ~10 s.** Not just a question: someone who wanted something or stood to lose something (the gangs vs the householder; the city vs the kids). One plain clause is enough. No invented drama.
- **But / so between beats, never "and then".** Read the beats in order and join them with "but" or "so" (South Park's rule). A beat that only joins with "and then" is a list item: cut it, or find the real turn and say it ("But the kids didn't stop.").
- **A mid-Short re-hook.** Around the middle (25-40 s), where attention drops, close one small question and open the next in the same line ("The men gave up their parades. The kids didn't."). Never leave a beat where the viewer feels finished before the end.
- **The head fake must be "of course" in hindsight.** The surprise is planted earlier in the script, so it feels fair, not random. (Already our accuracy rule: the twist is the true cause.)
- **Rhythm.** Put each sentence on its own line; if the line lengths all look the same, vary them: one short line after a long one lands the turn.
- **Show while you tell.** The first frame shows the thing the question is about, before the first word lands.
We don't take the casino framing literally: no fake cliffhangers, no withheld answers, no manufactured outrage. Calm and honest beats compulsive.

## Look
- Black-and-white line art on warm paper, lots of empty space.
- One accent color appears as **real paint**. It splashes onto the thing that matters, then lifts off and jumps to the next thing as the scene changes. The paint carries the chain, so the narration never needs to say "chain."
- No on-screen text except the cover frame, captions, the opening question (hook test) and hand-written numbers (`scene.letters`: a year, a price, a count, written on in our own pen strokes as it is spoken; at most one or two per Short, in scenes of 4 s or more) (2026-11-10).
- The paint is red lead #D9431E, the channel's signature.
- **The paint takes the color we call out (Matthew, 2026-10-07; made a firm rule 2026-10-08).** Whenever the narration, the title or the cover draws attention to a thing's color, the paint on that thing turns that color. Red paint on something we call purple reads as a contradiction. In the video it turns the color where it sits ("the glass turns purple" while it fills the bottle), holds it, then turns back to red before or while it jumps on. On the cover, the splash is the color the title or cover names (`cover.color` in layout.json; Short 03's cover went purple on 2026-10-08); the brand dot stays red. When the paint only marks a thing (the iron specks on "green"), it stays red. Stains, footprints and specks keep the color they had. Red stays the signature everywhere else. Colors: `PAINT_COLORS` in engine/plan.cjs (violet is manganese violet, a real manganese pigment; lavender; pale; wine; green); set per landing with `tint` in layout.json. Check before every cut and cover: each color the narration, title or cover calls out has the paint in that color.
- **Red paint must not read as blood (2026-10-19).** On 08 a red splat at the foot of the laxative bottle drew "why are we bleeding?" (5+ likes). On beats about the body, medicine, injury or danger (poison, blades, laxatives, wounds), don't land red on skin, cut food, blades or anything that could bleed; skip that landing or put it on a neutral object, and use a calm splat there (`'splat': {'nSpk': 0, 'nSat': 0, 'drip': 0}` in the layout's PAINT extras). Check every cut for this along with the color check. Red stays the signature.

## Hook and packaging
- **Test for Shorts 18-22 (Matthew, 2026-11-10, from the Fable review in research/REVIEW-fable-2026-11-10.md):**
  - Open on the strangest true fact of the story, not the mild question ("In 1939, America had two Thanksgivings"). The question follows in the first few seconds.
  - Show the question in big cover-style type on screen for the first ~1.5 s, with the paint already landing. (This is the one on-screen title; no branding at the start.)
  - Aim for 45-55 s (about 110-130 words), at least one character with a face in the first scene, and bigger paint (fill whole shapes) where it doesn't read as blood.
  - Premise test before research: would the title alone make someone say "wait, what?" Favor people in conflict and upcoming holidays, posted 1-2 weeks ahead.
  - Endings (2026-11-10): no film-reel outro or brand sting; end clean and short with the paint fading out. Seamless loops (first frame = last) are paused, since the on-screen question makes the first frame differ; revisit if Short 17's retention shows rewatching.
  - Judge on the run of five, mainly stayed-to-watch (goal: 70%+ on 3 of 5) and avg % viewed, not on one Short.
- Open on the question itself, asked directly. No "Have you ever wondered." (Paused for the test above.)
- Then name the unexpected other end within the first ~10 s (the promise).
- Titles ≤70 chars, no numbers or series names, never give away the payoff.
- Cover frame: 2–4 big words plus a brand mark in a fixed spot.
- **The title and cover never answer the question (Matthew, 2026-10-09).** Seen together, they should make the question bigger, not settle it. "Why are maraschino cherries so red? It's not the cherry" with "Bleached. Then dyed." gives the answer away, so a viewer can skip the Short. Better: a cover that adds a second puzzle (05: "Why are oranges orange? It went to the Supreme Court" with "Ripe. Still green.").

// the keyed word is the truth: if a paint time doesn't sit on its word, snap it to the word
function snapPaint(shots, words) {
  const warnings = [];
  for (const sc of shots.scenes) for (const p of sc.paint) {
    if (p.word == null) continue;   // a landing with no word (a thread's title card) keeps its time
    const key = normalize(p.word);
    const cands = words.filter(w => normalize(w.w) === key && Math.abs(w.s - p.t) < 1.5).sort((a, b) => Math.abs(a.s - p.t) - Math.abs(b.s - p.t));
    if (!cands.length) { warnings.push(`paint "${p.word}" @${p.t}: word not found near that time; using the time as given`); continue; }
    if (Math.abs(cands[0].s - p.t) > 0.04) {
      warnings.push(`paint "${p.word}" @${p.t}: that time is "${(words.find(w => Math.abs(w.s - p.t) < 0.04) || {}).w || '?'}"; snapped to "${cands[0].w}" @${cands[0].s}`);
      p.key_t = p.t; p.t = cands[0].s;
    }
  }
  return warnings;
}
