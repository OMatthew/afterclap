# Short 12 research (Halloween special): "Why do we carve pumpkins on Halloween?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-22 for a Halloween post. Research only, no script yet (a draft narration shape is at the end).
**Candidate:** Halloween special. Working title "Why do we carve pumpkins on Halloween?"; alternate hook "Why is it called a jack-o'-lantern?".
**Verdict:** **the question is safe, and the true answer beats the usual one: the carved lantern started as an autumn prank, not a Halloween thing, and Halloween picked it up later.** Documented, in order: (1) the *name* "jack-o'-lantern" is from the 1660s and meant a night watchman and the ghostly marsh lights; (2) a candle in a hollowed turnip, cut like a face, was a scare-prank in England by 1640 and was still a boys' prank in Worcestershire around 1800, where it was called "Hoberdy's lantern," the local name for the marsh light; (3) in America it was a pumpkin, and by 1838 a Boston children's book explains how to make one with no mention of Halloween; (4) for decades the American pumpkin lantern turns up at Thanksgiving, at election rallies and, in Portsmouth, N.H., on Guy Fawkes night (Nov. 5); (5) Harper's Weekly in 1867 called it a fading English import with no Halloween in it; (6) only in the 1880s do American magazines file it under Halloween. **Three corrections to the brief:** (1) **Hawthorne 1837 does not date the pumpkin.** "Look like a jack-o'-lantern" is a simile about a glowing gem seen through holes in a cloak; no pumpkin, no turnip (text read). Dictionary "1837/1834" first dates for the carved sense are not shown by that line. National Geographic's "Feathertop" claim is also unsupported: Feathertop has a carved pumpkin head, but the word "lantern" never appears in it (text read). (2) **"The Famine Irish brought it" is not supported.** Pumpkin lanterns are in a Boston children's book in 1838, before the Famine (1845–52); Harper's (1867) says the custom came from England; the Irish National Museum's folklife curator says of the Irish link "I don't think we are going to find the direct connection." (3) **The Stingy Jack legend is not as old or as turnip-y as claimed.** The oldest Irish version we could open (Dublin Penny Journal, Jan. 16, 1836) has no turnip, no coal, no coin, no apple tree and no "Stingy"; Jack is a churlish farmer who tricks the devil with an angel's three wishes, is barred from heaven and hell, and is "decreed to walk the earth with a lantern." The widely printed "mid-18th century" and "at least 1551" dates have no source behind them that we could find. **Recommended hook:** "Why do we carve pumpkins on Halloween?" with the line "It started as a prank, and Halloween came later."

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

Before it was a carved vegetable, "jack-o'-lantern" was a person and a light. By the 1660s it meant a man carrying a lantern at night, a night watchman (Etymonline: 1660s; OED via Wikipedia: 1663–1704), and a local name for the glimmering lights over bogs that misled night travelers (*ignis fatuus*, "will-o'-the-wisp"): Merriam-Webster's earliest date for that sense is 1667; Etymonline says 1670s. People explained the lights as ghosts or doomed souls; the natural explanation (gas from rotting plants catching fire) was already in print in 1596 and is still the best guess today (phosphine and methane, "scientists propose"). The Dublin Penny Journal of Jan. 16, 1836 stages the argument perfectly: a nephew says the light is "a gaseous vapour arising from putrid vegetable bodies," and his uncle, near tears, answers that it's a poor soul "doomed to wander with a lantern in his hand... until the day of judgment," then tells the legend of a churl named Jack.

The scary lantern face is a separate thing, and it is older than any Halloween link we can find. In a letter dated May 2, 1640, James Howell quotes a lord's saying that people who fear an uprising "are like boys and women that are afraid of a turnip cut like a death's head, with a candle in it." The Worcestershire antiquary Jabez Allies (born 1787) wrote that in his youth he saw peasant boys make a "Hoberdy's Lantern" by "hollowing out a turnip, and cutting eyes, nose, and mouth therein... the stump of a candle... place it upon a hedge to frighten unwary travellers in the night," and in the same book he says "Hoberdy's Lantern" and "Jack-o'-Lantern" were Worcestershire names for the marsh light. So there the carved lantern carried the marsh light's name. In Shropshire (printed 1883) the turnip lantern is in the hand of the Guy Fawkes effigy at Ludlow's Nov. 5 bonfire procession. Nothing in these ties it to Halloween or Samhain.

In America the vegetable was a pumpkin, and it was a farm-kid thing first. Jacob Abbott's *Rollo's Vacation* (copyright 1838) has Rollo ask the hired man Jonas, "what are jack-o'-lanterns?" and Jonas answer: "we take a pumpkin and dig it all out inside, and then cut eyes and nose and mouth in it, and then put a candle in and carry it out in the dark, and it makes a great grinning face of fire." The scene is corn harvest; Halloween isn't in the book. Over the next decades the pumpkin lantern shows up as a boy's autumn toy (Connecticut Common School Journal, Feb. 1842: "You have seen pumpkin lanterns—yes, and made them too"; Lowell's *Biglow Papers*, 1848: "something more than a pumpkin-lantern is required to scare"), in Whittier's boyhood Thanksgiving memory ("wild, ugly faces we carved in its skin, / Glaring out through the dark with a candle within!", in print by 1847), as torches in a Boston boys' election-night parade (Nov. 9, 1872: pumpkin-lanterns, "three more [cheers] for Grant"), and, in Portsmouth and New Castle, N.H., as the lights boys carried on Pope Night / Guy Fawkes night, Nov. 5 (John Albee, *Journal of American Folklore*, 1893: "Formerly the lights used by the boys... were candles set in hollowed-out pumpkins"; Portsmouth newspaper, Nov. 7, 1892: "Some carried the usual pumpkin lanterns"). Harper's Weekly's "The Pumpkin Effigy" (Nov. 23, 1867) says the "pastime came to this country from England" and compares it to the Guy Fawkes turnips; it says the sport was "now but seldom practiced." It never says Halloween. The same year Kingston, Ontario's *Daily News* (Nov. 1, 1866, via Wikipedia) reports "a great sacrifice of pumpkins from which to make transparent heads and face" on Hallowe'en night, and by Oct. 27, 1885 Harper's Young People tells children that in place of Scotland's bonfires "American boys delight in the funny grinning jack-o'-lanterns made of huge yellow pumpkins with a candle inside" at Halloween. Historians (Bannatyne, via TIME and GBH) say Americans largely reinvented Halloween as a party night after the Civil War, and Cindy Ott says harvest decorations (pumpkins central) were folded into Halloween. The American Agriculturist (Nov. 1880) was still describing "the Pumpkin Lantern, or Jack-Lantern" as a farm boy's autumn prank with no Halloween mention.

Why the carved lantern ended up with the name is not documented. Merriam-Webster calls the pranks theory "the most compelling" but speculative; the Worcestershire "Hoberdy's lantern" is the one case we found where a carved lantern is documented sharing the marsh light's name.

**Recommended chain** (six beats, ~65 s; beat 3's Worcestershire clause or beat 4's New Hampshire clause is the cut if long)
1. ★ **The question, and the answer in one line:** it started as a prank, and Halloween came later.
2. ★ **The name is older than the pumpkin.** 1660s: a man with a lantern; a ghostly marsh light that lured travelers.
3. ★ **The scary face came separately.** English boys hollowed turnips, put in a candle, set them on hedges to scare people walking home (1640 saying; Allies); in Worcestershire they used the marsh light's name.
4. ★ **In America it was a pumpkin, and not a Halloween thing.** An 1838 children's book explains it; boys carried them on Guy Fawkes night in Portsmouth, N.H.
5. ★ **Then Halloween grabbed it.** By 1885 a magazine lists grinning pumpkin lanterns as what American boys make for Halloween.
6. ★ **Last beat: the legend explains the lantern, not the pumpkin.** The oldest Irish Jack story we can find (Dublin, 1836) has no turnip. (This takes on the groove directly.)

## Hedges the narration must use
- **Name:** "In the 1660s" (not "1663" flat; dictionaries differ). "A man carrying a lantern" or "a night watchman" is right; say "ghostly lights over marshes" for the other sense.
- **Marsh lights:** "scientists think it's gas from rotting plants" (a hypothesis), never "it's methane."
- **Turnips in England:** "In England, boys hollowed out turnips..." rests on Allies (one county, one memory) plus the 1640 saying. If cut to one claim, say "an English antiquarian remembered..." or keep it on Worcestershire.
- **"Same name as the marsh light":** only for Worcestershire ("Hoberdy's lantern"); never "turnip lanterns were called jack-o'-lanterns".
- **1838:** "A children's book from 1838" / "by 1838." Not "the first pumpkin jack-o'-lantern" and not "the first mention."
- **Halloween:** "In the 1880s American magazines list it as a Halloween thing" / "By 1885 a magazine said...". Never "since the 1800s on Halloween" flat. Kingston 1866 is Canadian.
- **Guy Fawkes night:** one town (Portsmouth, N.H.), 1890s, "boys carried them on Guy Fawkes night" (also called Pope Night; New Castle boys said "Pork Night"). Explain in a clause: an English holiday on November fifth.
- **The Irish tale:** "an Irish story printed in Dublin in 1836"; "the oldest version we can find." Never "Stingy Jack," "ancient Irish legend," or "the Irish invented it."
- **Irish immigrants:** if mentioned at all, "no one has shown that Irish immigrants brought it." Better to leave out.
- **Causes:** "partly because," "historians say." The Halloween adoption has no single document.
- **Avoid "chain"**; at most one question pivot (the closing "And the Irish tale of Jack...?" uses it; keep the others plain).

## Myths to avoid
1. **"The Celts carved turnips 2,000 years ago / at Samhain."** Suggested by a museum curator as a possible root; no documented line (NatGeo; IrishCentral). Dr David Clarke calls turnip carving "a fairly recent custom." The earliest turnip-and-candle scare we found is 1640 England.
2. **"Stingy Jack, a blacksmith who tricked the devil twice, got a coal in a turnip" is an old, authentic Irish legend about the jack-o'-lantern.** The tale type is international (Newell 1904); the oldest Irish text we opened (1836) has no turnip; "mid-18th century" and "1551" are unsourced; "Stingy" is in none of our old texts.
3. **"Irish immigrants fleeing the Famine brought it and swapped turnips for pumpkins."** Pumpkin lanterns are in a Boston children's book in 1838, years before the Famine; Harper's (1867) says it came from England; the Irish curator says no direct connection will be found. (Ott's own estimate "1860s or 1870s" for the first pumpkin jack-o'-lanterns, via WDEL 2017, is contradicted by the 1838 and 1842 texts, which she may mean as images; the Harper's 1867 engraving is the first *image* we know.)
4. **"Hawthorne's 'The Great Carbuncle' or 'Feathertop' is the first pumpkin jack-o'-lantern."** The 1837 line is a simile about a gem seen through holes; *Feathertop* never says it. (National Geographic, All That's Interesting, Mental Floss and others repeat the claim.)
5. **"It was always a Halloween thing."** For decades it was a general autumn prank, also used at Thanksgiving, on Nov. 5, and at election rallies (see table).
6. **"Washington Irving's Headless Horseman gave us the jack-o'-lantern."** The pumpkin in *Sleepy Hollow* (1820) is a thrown pumpkin, not a lantern (Irish Times: "isn't clear whether it was carved"; Morton and Skal via Wikipedia).
7. **"Somerset's Punkie Night shows turnip lanterns are an ancient British Halloween custom."** First record 1931.
8. **"Most US pumpkins are grown for carving."** Not verified; Illinois alone grows over a quarter of the US crop, mostly canning varieties (NASA 2021).
9. **"The Irish carved turnips at Halloween to ward off Stingy Jack."** The ward-off-spirits purpose is a modern explanation (curators: lanterns "were being made in Ireland"); no old text says why.

## Groove risk
- **What generic AI Shorts say:** "Long ago in Ireland, a trickster named Stingy Jack fooled the Devil twice. When he died, neither Heaven nor Hell would take him, so the Devil gave him a coal, and he carried it in a hollowed-out turnip. The Irish carved scary faces into turnips to scare him off. When Irish immigrants came to America, they found pumpkins were easier to carve, and a tradition was born." Sometimes with Samhain, Celts and "ward off evil spirits." Nearly every listicle says this (History.com, Smithsonian Gardens, Farmers' Almanac, NatGeo, TIME, Mental Floss).
- **How we differ:** open with the answer ("It started as a prank, and Halloween came later"), then the name that came first (night watchman, marsh lights), the turnip prank in England, the 1838 how-to with no Halloween, Guy Fawkes night in New Hampshire, and the 1885 line when Halloween finally claimed it. End by taking on the Stingy Jack story: the oldest Irish text we can find has no turnip. **No Celts, no Samhain, no Famine, no "Stingy Jack," no "ward off spirits."**
- **Visual groove to avoid:** witches, bats, black cats, symmetrical clip-art pumpkin faces, glowing neon pumpkins, Celtic knots, cartoon devils. Skip skulls too (the 1640 "death's head" can be a bare turnip with simple eyes).

## Story shape (proposed, ~180 words, ~65–70 s)

**Hook (in the first 10 s):** "Why do we carve pumpkins on Halloween?" Stakes in the first beats: someone being startled in the dark.

**Draft narration v1 (186 words with the hook; plain, one question pivot, no "chain"):**

> Why do we carve pumpkins on Halloween?
> It started as a prank, and Halloween came later.
> The name is older than the pumpkin. In the 1660s, a jack-o'-lantern was a man carrying a lantern at night. It also meant the ghostly lights over marshes that lured travelers astray.
> The scary face came separately. In England, boys hollowed out turnips, lit a candle inside, and set them on hedges to frighten people walking home. In Worcestershire, they used the marsh lights' own name for them.
> In America, they used pumpkins. An 1838 children's book has a farmhand explain it: dig it out, cut eyes, a nose, a mouth, put a candle in, and carry it into the dark. No Halloween in it. In Portsmouth, New Hampshire, boys carried them on Guy Fawkes night, an English holiday on November fifth.
> Halloween grabbed the idea later. By 1885, a magazine said American boys made grinning pumpkin lanterns for it.
> And the Irish tale of Jack, the devil and a turnip? The oldest version we can find, printed in 1836, has no turnip. It explains the lantern. Not the pumpkin.

**Notes for the lead**
- **But/so check:** it started as a prank, *but* nobody tied it to Halloween; the name was old, *so* the scare lantern got borrowed names; *but* Halloween finally claimed it. The mid re-hook is "No Halloween in it," then the Guy Fawkes line pays off "prank."
- **Cuts if long:** the Worcestershire clause (beat 3), then the Portsmouth sentence (beat 4). The 1660s line and the 1836 ending carry the "wait, what" and should stay.
- **Plain-language pass (spots a general listener may need twice):** "jack-o'-lantern" in the 1660s (say it means a person first), "lured travelers astray" (fine), "Guy Fawkes night" (explained in a clause), "1836" (say "a Dublin magazine in 1836" if the ear needs it), "the lantern" vs "the pumpkin" in the last two sentences (pronouns: "It explains the lantern" = the Jack story explains the *light*; consider "That story explains the glowing lantern. Not the pumpkin.").
- **Alternate ending (verified, optional swap for beat 6):** "And the pumpkins we carve now? The first one sold mainly for carving came out in 1973." (Howden; Fedco, CooksInfo, Purdue). It's a fresh fact but a topic change; keep the Jack ending unless the lead wants it lighter.
