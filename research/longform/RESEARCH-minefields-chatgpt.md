## Research fact-check: "Why Europe Is Planting Minefields Again"
Research verified through 13 November 2026

**Verdict: This is a strong documentary premise, with an unusually compelling historical opening.** The central story is real: Europe spent decades removing the weapons and explosive remnants of old wars, while five NATO countries have now abandoned the international ban on anti-personnel mines and are preparing to acquire them again.

Four corrections before scripting:

- **Europe is preparing to lay new minefields, not necessarily planting them already.** Fortifications are being built and mine procurement is advancing, but no verified evidence of newly laid, permanent anti-personnel minefields along the Polish or Baltic borders.
- **The Messines mines were not modern landmines.** They were enormous underground demolition charges, detonated deliberately beneath enemy positions. Modern anti-personnel mines are generally triggered by a person's presence, proximity or contact. The film needs to distinguish them.
- **The five European countries have already legally withdrawn** (December 2025 to February 2026). Ukraine announced a withdrawal but instead attempted to suspend its obligations, a move whose legality is disputed.
- **Defense has become extremely powerful in Ukraine, but the idea that it has permanently defeated offense is contested.** That disagreement is a good ending for the film.

Preferred working title: "Why Europe Is Preparing Minefields Again." The existing title is more arresting but needs the qualification early in the narration.

## 2. Messines: 7 June 1917
Battle established; abandoned-charge count requires care.

| Detail | Verified finding |
|---|---|
| Date | 7 June 1917 |
| Time | 3:10 a.m. local time |
| British commander | General Herbert Plumer, Second Army |
| Detonations | 19 enormous underground mines |
| Explosives | approx. 1 million lb (roughly 450-455 metric tonnes) of ammonal |
| Frontage | approx. 10 km / six miles |
| Objective | Destroy German strongpoints and capture Messines-Wytschaete Ridge south of Ypres |
| Outcome | British and Commonwealth forces captured the ridge; German counterattacks failed |

The Australian government's military-history portal confirms the date, time, 19 explosions and tactical outcome. The Anzac Memorial records more than one million pounds of explosive.

The 19 were not simultaneous: Simon Jones documents timing differences of up to about 30 seconds.

Two claims to treat with caution:
- "The largest explosion before the atomic bomb" is too absolute. Among the largest coordinated conventional demolitions of its era, but other pre-1945 explosions complicate the ranking.
- "10,000 Germans died instantly" is an uncertain historical estimate, not a verified casualty count.

### How many mines were left behind?
Jones identifies six substantial charges abandoned in 1917. One detonated in 1955, leaving five believed to remain.

| Site | Status | Historical charge |
|---|---|---|
| Birdcage, near Le Pèlerin | Four initially abandoned; one exploded in 1955 | Four charges originally totalling 112,000 lb |
| La Petite Douve Farm, later La Basse Cour | British charge abandoned after German countermining disrupted its tunnel in Aug 1916 | 50,000 lb / 22.7 t |
| Peckham Farm, gallery C | Abandoned after flooding and tunnel collapse | 20,000 lb / 9.1 t |

The three surviving Birdcage charges plus La Petite Douve and Peckham make five believed-unexploded charges, roughly 70 tonnes of explosive remaining (historical estimates).

Why books say 21, 22, 24 or 25 mines: some count planned mines or tunnels rather than completed charges; some exclude charges made unusable by German countermining; some omit the four Birdcage charges because they were outside the final attack area; some count only mines intended for 7 June.

Suggested formulation: "19 detonated, six substantial charges were left behind, one exploded in 1955," attributed to Jones's archival research.

**What Belgian authorities say today:** Belgium officially acknowledges the continued risk of buried wartime explosives; DOVO/SEDEE handles disposal and Flemish authorities maintain procedures for evaluating buried ordnance before excavation. NOT verified: any current official Belgian inventory confirming the condition of all five Messines charges, or an official decision declaring them safe indefinitely. The five-charge figure is a historical reconstruction, not a modern inspection. Belgium's heritage inventory confirms an old German countermine shaft at La Basse Cour; that is not the buried British charge itself (matters for maps and illustrations).

Sources: Simon Jones, The Lost Mines; Flemish Heritage Agency (La Basse Cour); Belgian Defence EOD.

## 3. The iron harvest: France and Belgium

| Country | Annual quantity | Source / qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Belgium | More than 200 tonnes | DOVO's official annual average |
| France | approx. 450-500 tonnes | Typical annual scale per French government sources |
| France, 2024 | 358 tonnes | Government 2024 performance report |
| France, Jan-Jul 2025 | 257 tonnes | Interim figure, not full-year |

Belgium's foreign ministry also says around 200 tonnes of unexploded WWI ordnance are found annually in the Westhoek region. Different coverage and definitions; do not add them together.

People still killed:
- **18 April 2007, Moselle, France:** two French bomb-disposal officers, Dominique Milesi and Laurent Flauder, died handling WWI grenades at a disposal facility near Ressaincourt.
- **19 March 2014, Ypres, Belgium:** two construction workers died and two were injured when a WWI shell exploded during excavation. A 2024 court ruling found individuals and companies guilty of involuntary manslaughter.
- Belgian State Archives also document a farmer severely injured in 1921, plus compensation cases from 1992 and 2011.
- No adequately documented recent farmer fatality found; use the deminers and construction workers instead.

**Zone rouge:** After the war France designated heavily devastated battlefield areas as the "zone rouge." Law of 17 April 1919 let the state acquire land unfit for ordinary restoration. Original designation approx. 120,000 hectares (boundaries and restrictions have changed). Verdun is the clearest example. Do not say the whole original zone is still legally closed; much has been restored, reused or opened for controlled visits.

Sources: Belgian Defence; French Interior Ministry (deminers); France's 2024 performance report; Belgian State Archives; French Ministry of Defence.

### Why armies went underground
The explanation is substantially right. The National Army Museum names machine guns, rapid-firing artillery and barbed wire as major causes of stalemate. Add: defenders could often bring reinforcements forward faster than attackers could move artillery, ammunition and supplies across destroyed ground. Trenches answered being observed and shot at; tunnelling became a way past those defenses. Messines is interesting because the underground attack actually worked.

## 4. Global landmines: human cost and clearance time
Latest comprehensive report: Landmine Monitor 2025, released 1 December 2025.

2024 confirmed casualties worldwide: **6,279** (1,945 killed, 4,325 injured, 9 unknown), about 90% civilians where status known. Includes unexploded cluster submunitions.

Most recorded casualties in 2024: Myanmar 2,029; Syria 1,015; Afghanistan 624; Ukraine 293; Nigeria 275; Mali 268; Yemen 265.

A June 2026 preliminary release found more than 5,000 casualties during 2025 (Syria 1,602; Myanmar 745; Afghanistan 497). Incomplete minimum figures, not directly comparable. Next full Landmine Monitor scheduled 25 November 2026.

| Country | Situation |

| Angola | Still clearing; requested extension to 2030, about $197.5 million proposed for 2026-30 |

## 5. The 1997 Ottawa Treaty and Princess Diana

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 18 Sep 1997 | Treaty adopted at Oslo |
| 3-4 Dec 1997 | Opened for signature in Ottawa; 122 states signed |
| 1 Mar 1999 | Entered into force |
| Dec 2025 - Feb 2026 | Five European countries completed withdrawals |
| 1 Nov 2026 | Lebanon's accession took effect |
| 13 Nov 2026 | 162 states parties |

Prohibits use, production, stockpiling and transfer of anti-personnel mines; requires destruction and clearance. Does not generally prohibit anti-vehicle mines or operator-detonated explosives.

Never joined: United States, Russia, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea, South Korea, Israel.

The ban movement had momentum before Diana. The ICBL and Jody Williams won the 1997 Nobel Peace Prize.

## 6. Ukraine
Official figures verified; suspected area is not confirmed minefield.

- Ukraine's government (Jan 2025): potentially contaminated area fell from about 174,000 km2 to 139,000 km2.
- UNMAS calls Ukraine the world's most extensive current explosive-ordnance contamination challenge; mid-2025 estimate more than 139,000 km2.
- Ukraine's national mine-action platform now shows about 132,000 km2 under risk (explanatory text keeps older estimates).
- These are suspected areas needing assessment; some can be released after survey without removing explosives.

**Cost:** Feb 2026 World Bank/Ukraine/EU/UN assessment (RDNA5, 23 Feb 2026): **$27.6 billion** for explosive hazards management and debris clearance, 2026-2035; a ten-year requirement, not a firm price or timetable. Older $37B+ figures are outdated. "700 years" is not a dependable forecast.

**2023 counteroffensive:** Russia built layered defenses: AT and AP minefields, trenches, AT ditches, concrete obstacles, artillery and observation, attack helicopters and drones. RUSI documented minefield depth growing from doctrinal approx. 120 m to practical approx. 500 m in some sectors. Ukraine declared Robotyne liberated 28 August 2023 but did not break through toward Tokmak and Melitopol. RUSI's later investigation cites multiple causes: delayed Western equipment and ammunition, operational security, command decisions, breaching-capability deficiencies, Russian adaptation. Minefields were a major obstacle, not the sole cause.

**US AP mines decision:** 20 November 2024, Biden administration confirmed it would supply Ukraine with non-persistent anti-personnel mines to counter Russian infantry assaults. Self-deactivating AP mines are still prohibited by the Ottawa Treaty. Landmine Monitor documents evidence indicating Ukrainian use of AP mines, including drone-delivered, though scale cannot be independently established.

Sources: US State Dept briefing 20 Nov 2024; RUSI Sept 2023; Landmine Monitor Ukraine; World Bank RDNA5.

## 7. Treaty withdrawals and European defense lines
Dates confirmed by the UN treaty depositary.

| Country | Announced | UN notification | Legally effective |
|---|---|---|---|
| Estonia | 18 Mar 2025 | 27 Jun 2025 | 27 Dec 2025 |
| Latvia | 18 Mar 2025 | 27 Jun 2025 | 27 Dec 2025 |
| Lithuania | 18 Mar 2025 | 27 Jun 2025 | 27 Dec 2025 |
| Finland | 1 Apr 2025 | 10 Jul 2025 | 10 Jan 2026 |
| Poland | 18 Mar 2025 | 20 Aug 2025 | 20 Feb 2026 |

The three Baltic states and Poland issued a joint defense-ministerial recommendation on 18 March 2025. All five cited the deteriorating security environment and Russia's military threat. First completed state withdrawals from the treaty.

**Ukraine is different:** 29 June 2025 Zelenskyy signed a decree supporting withdrawal. 17 July 2025 Ukraine instead purported to suspend the treaty's operation (UN communication circulated 21 July), arguing Russia's mine use created unacceptable military disadvantage. Article 20 stops a withdrawal taking effect while the state is in armed conflict. At the December 2025 meeting, states parties affirmed the convention does not permit Ukraine's suspension. Ukraine remains legally a state party; don't list it with the five.

**On the borders:**
- **Estonia, Baltic Defence Line:** up to 600 bunkers, anti-tank ditches, barriers, forward storage. First structures installed (southeast Estonia, near Narva). Government describes mines as a potential wartime measure, not a permanent peacetime border feature.
- **Latvia:** EUR 303 million, five-year plan: anti-tank trenches, fortified posts, ammunition/mine storage. Construction underway. Feb 2026 agreement toward a domestic anti-tank mine factory, expected 2028.
- **Lithuania:** planning minefield groups, acquiring anti-tank mines, has expressed plans to stockpile AP mines. June 2026: received anti-tank mines from Estonia. Planning is not proof of deployment.
- **Poland, East Shield:** launched May 2024: obstacles, surveillance, anti-drone systems, logistics. By August 2026 about 100 km of sensitive border sections reinforced. Preparing to produce and acquire AP mines; deployment if a serious threat emerges.
- **Finland:** pursuing domestic procurement of approx. one million AP mines, production targeted 2028. Defence Forces say they would be stockpiled in normal conditions, not laid on the border in peacetime.

**Last six months:**
- 21 May 2026: Polish manufacturer Niewiadow announced a partnership to develop and produce new AP mine types; October: reiterated Q4 2026 production timetable (manufacturing prep, not confirmed deliveries).
- 29 Sep 2026: Finland's Defence Forces issued supplier information requests for AP fragmentation and blast mines, potential procurement about EUR 100 million.
- 12 Oct 2026: ICBL urged governments to intervene over renewed Polish and Finnish AP mine production plans.
- 28 Oct 2026: Estonia's parliament removed an additional domestic prohibition on transport of AP mines.
- 30 Oct 2026: more Baltic Defence Line bunkers being installed near Narva (ERR).
None proves new peacetime AP minefields have been laid on these borders.

## 8. Drones, mines and whether defense is winning again
**Laying:** drones drop mines on routes and positions. Human Rights Watch documented Russian drones emplacing AP and AT mines around Kherson. Ukraine's MoD announced changes in Dec 2025 to widen production of drone-emplaced mines. Landmine Monitor records evidence of Ukrainian drone-delivered AP mines too.

**Finding/clearing:** survey drones with cameras, magnetometers and sensors map hazards. Ukraine's MoD described airborne magnetometer deployments in Mykolaiv and training in 2025-26. UNDP has supported AI recognition of explosive hazards in aerial imagery. Limit: spotting a mine is not removing it or certifying land safe; physical clearance still needs mechanical systems and trained teams.

**Experts:**

| View | Named expert / institution | Argument |
|---|---|---|
| Defense currently holds a structural advantage | Jack Watling, RUSI | Persistent surveillance, precision strikes, vulnerable supply routes undermine massed maneuver. Oct 2025 research also identifies emerging Ukrainian techniques to restore offensive capability |
| Drone revolution may fundamentally change warfare | Andriy Zagorodnyuk, Carnegie Endowment, Apr 2026 | Cheap networked precision weapons reward denial and dispersal; balance may not be permanent |
| Minefields and prepared defenses decisive in 2023 | RUSI; US Army War College | Dense obstacles plus artillery, helicopters, drones and weak breaching frustrated the offensive |
| Offense can adapt | Michael Kofman, Carnegie; ISW | Outcomes depend on adaptation, combined arms, resources, exploiting openings. ISW documented Ukrainian counterattacks and tactical initiative in parts of the front in 2026 |
| Technology doesn't decide the balance | Keir A. Lieber | Effects conditioned by doctrine, politics, geography; balance hard to measure; theory weak in some historical tests |

**Academic framework:** Robert Jervis, "Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma," World Politics, January 1978 (cooperation easier when defense has the advantage and defensive postures are distinguishable). Critique: Keir Lieber, "Grasping the Technological Peace," International Security, Summer 2000; book War and the Engineers (2005). Avoid treating offense-defense theory as a numeric measurement showing defense has "won."

Suggested framing question: has cheap, persistent surveillance made massed attacks so costly that European armies are redesigning their defenses, or is this a temporary phase before new tactics emerge?
