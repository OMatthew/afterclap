## Short answer

- **Pick: the Messines mines.** In 1955 lightning set off a First World War mine in a Belgian field. It was one of several the British army dug under a ridge in 1916-17 and never fired, and the biggest is still under a farm.
- **Runner-up: why the Mongols left Europe in 1242,** told as a line-up of four suspects.
- **Offense vs defense: not as video one.** It is the right spine for a series, and Messines is its strongest single scene. Make the thesis video third or fourth, when the drawings exist and there is retention data.

## The contenders, bluntly

### 7. Offense vs defense (Matthew's favourite)

What is good: the frame is rare on YouTube, it explains a lot, and every era is a Short.

What is wrong with it as the first video:

1. **It is a thesis, not a story.** There is no single person, place or question pulling a viewer from minute 2 to minute 8. Each jump to a new era is a natural place to leave. The survey's main lesson was one central question with connected turns, and Matthew's own note on the 01-09 film was "one cohesive narrative, not a hodgepodge". Five eras in ten minutes is two minutes each, which is five Shorts with linking lines.
2. **The claim as stated is partly wrong.** The theory (Jervis 1978, Van Evera) says war is more *likely* when attack looks easy. It does not say those eras are deadlier. The deadliest example in the list, 1914-18, is the one where defense had the advantage. "How deadly it is" would need rewriting before any script.
3. **The theory is contested.** Keir Lieber (International Security, 2000) looked at railroads, the small-arms and artillery revolution, tanks and nuclear weapons and concluded that technology only occasionally favors one side and that the perceived balance has little effect on whether wars start. Every era also has its own argument: how much the cannon mattered in 1453, whether a "cult of the offensive" caused 1914. Our accuracy rule (two sources for the central link, hedge causes) would hedge nearly every sentence.
4. **The groove check passes on the topic and fails on the treatment.** Castles, Constantinople, 1914, deterrence, drones is the standard list from an international-relations course. Any model asked for this video gives those five beats in that order.
5. **Cost.** Five periods means five sets of drawings (about 40-50), plus maps. The drones and AI ending dates fast and pulls us toward current-affairs opinion, even if we only report the disagreement.

### 4. Cold War nuclear close calls

The best click appeal on the list and the least original. Arkhipov and Petrov are on nearly every history channel. It is a list of four, so it is a compilation in structure. The famous versions are tidier than the record: the Arkhipov "one vote" account rests on recollections given decades later, and how dangerous Able Archer 83 really was is disputed among historians **[verify: Simon Miles, 2020, argues the scare was overstated]**. A sharper version exists ("the autumn of 1983" as one story), but it is still well covered. Good Shorts, weak first long video.

### 2. Mongols turn back, 1242 (runner-up)

Checked: Ögedei died on 11 December 1241 and the Mongols began leaving Hungary in late March 1242. Rashid al-Din says Batu did not know of the death when he decided to withdraw, and the news would have had to cross Asia in about three months in midwinter. Büntgen and Di Cosmo (Scientific Reports, 2016) used tree rings to argue that a cold, wet early 1242 turned the Hungarian plain to marsh and cut pasture. Pinke and colleagues replied in the same journal in 2017 ("Climate of doubt") with five objections, and the authors answered "we stand by our conclusions". Stephen Pow (Calgary thesis 2012, CEU dissertation 2020) argues that stone fortifications and stiff resistance were a real problem; all of Hungary's few stone castles held, Béla IV built 44 more in ten years, and the 1285 invasion failed.

Strengths: proven demand, a "why did they stop?" title of the History Matters kind, and a structure that writes itself (four suspects, each cleared or left standing, ending on a real disagreement). One suspect is castles, so it serves Matthew's theme. Tree rings suit the paint.

Weaknesses: it is well covered (Kings and Generals has several films on 1241-42 and SciShow has done the climate angle). Horses, armies and maps are the most expensive things for us to draw. Medieval sources need careful handling, and the death toll (perhaps 15-25% of Hungary's people) makes the red paint hard to place.

### 3. Project Azorian

The CIA built a ship under a Howard Hughes cover to lift the Soviet submarine K-129 from about 16,500 feet in 1974; most of it broke away during the lift; a reporter's records request then produced the phrase "neither confirm nor deny". The sources are the best on this list (the CIA's own history, released in 2010). It is the closest match to what Afterclap already does, an everyday phrase with a strange origin. But it is heavily covered, it is engineering more than conflict, and it is the mildest answer to "less vanilla".

### 5. Gulf of Tonkin

On 4 August 1964 two US destroyers reported an attack that an NSA history released in 2005 concluded never happened; Congress passed the war resolution days later. The paint as radar blips that are not there is a good idea. It is the most political topic here and the least visual, and the title is hard to write without giving the answer.

### 6. Constantinople 1453

The story that the gunfounder Orban offered his services to the emperor first comes mainly from one chronicler, Doukas **[verify]**. Very well covered, expensive to draw, and it works better as the second or third video in a series.

## The pick: the Messines mines

**Why it wins**

1. **It is one story with a built-in mystery.** It opens on a field exploding in peacetime, and the question "why was that there, and are there more?" holds until the last minute.
2. **It is Matthew's idea in one concrete scene.** By 1915 defense had won above ground: machine guns, wire and artillery stopped every attack on the ridge. So the British went underneath it. The thesis is carried by events and never has to be stated as theory.
3. **It is our format at the far end of the chain.** A farm today, back to a ridge in 1917, back to sewer diggers from Manchester. It is still a "why is this like this?" question.
4. **It suits the look.** Most of it is cross-sections: a ridge, tunnels, men listening in the dark. The paint is the buried charges, not blood: nineteen red marks under the ridge go off, and a few stay. The last frame is a farmhouse with one red mark deep below it, which is also the thumbnail.
5. **It is cheap for a long video.** Three settings (underground, the ridge, the farm) and about 25-30 drawings, many reused with zooms.
6. **The well-known versions contain myths we can correct,** which is our accuracy brand doing work on screen.

**Weaknesses**

- Less name recognition than the Mongols or nuclear war. The title has to carry it.
- Television has covered the tunnellers (PBS Nova, "WW1 Tunnels of Death"), and there are certainly YouTube videos. I could not measure how many or how big; the lead should scan YouTube before committing.
- The tunnelling middle (2:00-5:30) is the retention risk. It needs the underground duel, not a lecture on digging.
- The scale of death on 7 June 1917 has to be handled calmly and with honest numbers.
- The Nov 10 review advised against long-form until a Short breaks out. That still stands as a cost warning: cap this at one build, and count hours.

**Facts checked, and facts still to check**

- Checked: 19 mines were fired at 3:10 a.m. on 7 June 1917, about a million pounds of explosive in all (Simon Jones, historian of the tunnellers). More were laid than fired: four at "the Birdcage" near Ploegsteert were not needed, one at Peckham was lost to a collapse or flooding, and the one at Petit Douve farm (about 50,000 lb) was found by German counter-miners in August 1916 and abandoned. One Birdcage mine exploded in a thunderstorm in 1955 and killed a cow. The claim that Lloyd George heard the mines in England is doubtful: he was in Surrey, about 140 miles away, and Jones thinks he more likely heard the 2,000 guns; seismographs in Utrecht and on the Isle of Wight did register it.
- **[verify]** The 1955 date (17 June in one source; 17 July appears elsewhere). The total number laid (sources give 21 to 26, depending on what is counted). Whether lightning struck a pylon built over the mine. The often-quoted 10,000 German dead, which Jones has examined and which I could not read. The "change the geography" line and who said it (usually Harington, Plumer's chief of staff). John Norton-Griffiths and the Manchester "clay-kickers" (February 1915). The current state of the Petit Douve mine and the farm above it, and what Belgian authorities or experts say about the risk.

## Two title options

1. **Why is there still a WWI bomb under this farm?**
2. **Lightning set off a bomb from WWI. Another is still buried.**

Neither says why the mines were dug or why some were left. Thumbnail: a small inked farmhouse on the paper, a long empty drop, one red mark far below. No text, or two words ("STILL THERE").

## Opening 30 seconds (prose)

A summer evening in 1955, in farmland in the south of Belgium. A thunderstorm comes in over the fields. We see a flat field, a few cows and an electricity pylon, drawn small, with a lot of sky. Lightning comes down. Then the field itself goes up: earth thrown into the air, and a crater where there was grass. The narrator says, calmly, that nobody was hurt, apart from one cow. He says the explosive had been in the ground for thirty-eight years, that the British army had put it there on purpose, and that it was not the only one. The picture pulls down through the soil to show the ridge in cross-section, with red marks buried along it. Most fade out. A few stay. The question appears: why is there still a bomb under this farm?

## Shorts that could feed it

1. **"In 1955, lightning set off a bomb from the First World War."** The cold open as a 45 s Short, ending on "and it wasn't the only one". This is the entry point; set the long video as its related video.
2. **"Britain sent sewer diggers to fight the First World War."** The clay-kickers: who they were, how they dug, why silence mattered. People and a strange job, like Short 11.
3. **"Did the Prime Minister hear a battle from his bed in England?"** The myth check: the claim, where he actually was, the guns, the seismographs.

## If Matthew wants offense vs defense anyway

Make it the series, one hinge per video: Messines (defense wins, so go under), then the Mongols and the stone castles, then Constantinople and the gun. A fourth video can state the thesis, reuse those drawings, and end on what experts dispute. By then the claim can be worded correctly: easy attack makes war more likely, not more deadly.
