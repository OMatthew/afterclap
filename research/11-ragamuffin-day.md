# Short 11 research: "Why did New York kids once go door to door in costume on Thanksgiving?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-16 for a post around Oct 23. Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** the link out of 10, as recorded in 10's narration v3.1 ("And why did New York kids once go door to door in costume on Thanksgiving? That's another story."). 10's last scene: a kid in an oversized coat and hat, holding the hat out at a door with a paper turkey; the paint lands on the turkey.
**Verdict:** **the tease is safe as written.** From at least the 1880s to the 1930s, New York children dressed up on Thanksgiving and asked for pennies on the street and at doors ("Anything for Thanksgiving?"). A 1903 New York Times editorial complained about "ringing all the doorbells and demanding backsheesh" (Kawash 2011; Mental Floss 2016). "Once" is right: the begging was gone by about 1940. **The true answer to "why" is better than the usual one: they were copying the grown-ups.** A Brooklyn pastor in 1909: "About the year 1870, on Thanksgiving Day, the different societies in New York dressed in elegant costumes, paraded the streets and made social calls. The children, quick to see a chance for fun, began to imitate the grown-ups. Dressing in old clothes, many sizes too large, painting their faces or putting on masks, the children went out to mimic the seniors. The grown-ups have given up their custom, but the children keep up the imitation" (New-York Tribune, Nov. 22, 1909, read from the page image). The Sun said the same in 1907: the grown men's "parades of fantasticals ... have gone," and what's left is "a strange survival of the old custom" in children who "hold up all they meet for a penny for Thanksgiving." **The conflict:** then the adults turned on it. Editorials called it an abuse (1903), the police were ordered to discourage the begging (1922), the schools superintendent said "modernity is incompatible" with it (1930), and a boys' club ran a parade under the slogan "American boys do not beg." **The far end:** the begging was gone by about 1940, and within a decade a new custom reached New York from the West: kids in costume ringing doorbells for treats, on Halloween (Kawash). The same complaints came back. Today Brooklyn's Ragamuffin Parade (since 1966) still has kids marching in their parents' oversized clothes, in October. **Three corrections to the brief:** (1) The slogan is **"American boys do not beg,"** not "don't." Its year is **1940** (NYPL blog, then NPR and 6sqft) or **1942** (Kawash, citing NYT Nov. 22, 1942). Atlas Obscura's "1937" is the year the boys' club parade *began*, not the slogan. Say "by the early 1940s" or give no year. (2) **The Macy's parade did not grow out of Ragamuffin Day.** No source says it did. Several say the Macy's parade (from 1924) *overshadowed* the ragamuffin parades. Reject a cause link; keep "overshadowed." (3) **Ragamuffin Day was not the origin of trick-or-treating.** Tuleja calls it "the most plausible 'missing link'," but Kawash finds "no evidence that the actual practice of trick-or-treating originated in New York"; it "appears to have arrived in the East quite late, sometime in the late 1940s," from the West.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

In 19th-century New York, Thanksgiving wasn't a quiet family holiday. Grown men's clubs, the "target companies" and "fantasticals," paraded in costume, some on horseback and some in their sisters' dresses, then went off to picnics. By the 1880s, Brooklyn papers described "bands of small boys dressed up in fantastic garbs, beating tin pans and blowing discordant fish horns" all day (Tribune, Nov. 28, 1884). A pastor dated the children's version to "about the year 1870": the kids imitated the grown-ups, "dressing in old clothes, many sizes too large." The grown-ups' parades faded by the 1890s–1900s; the kids' version grew ("The practice of dressing up as ragamuffins seems to be growing among the city children each Thanksgiving Day," Tribune 1902), and it added a request: "Anything for Thanksgiving?" Pennies, an apple, a piece of candy. Before Halloween meant masks, Thanksgiving was the mask-makers' busiest season (LA Times 1897, via NPR 2014; NYPL's Carmen Nigro, NPR 2025). Adults soured on it. The Times in 1903: "The practice of ringing all the doorbells and demanding backsheesh is long past a joke. It is an abuse which should be restrained." The Sun in 1907 headlined "Nuisance of Small Beggars ... Degeneration of the Old Fantastical Into a Squad of Saucy Mendicants," and an old New Yorker told it, "Child beggars on Thanksgiving Day are no more a custom of old New York than is the riot down at Trinity Church on New Year's Eve." In 1922 the police department's Chief Inspector ordered officers to "discourage it as much as possible," and the Herald mourned: "New York Loses an Institution." In 1930 the schools superintendent wrote that "modernity is incompatible with the custom of children to masquerade and annoy adults on Thanksgiving day." The Depression made begging look like the real thing, and from 1937 the Madison Square Boys Club ran a costume parade "to discourage the Thanksgiving ragamuffins," with the slogan "American boys do not beg." By 1940 the Times' Thanksgiving coverage no longer mentioned children begging (Kawash); the parades lasted into the 1950s (last Times mention 1956, in the Bronx), overshadowed by Macy's. Meanwhile trick-or-treating spread east from places like Washington, Oregon and Indiana and reached the East Coast in the late 1940s. And the same complaints came back on Oct. 31: a Washington Post letter writer wanted the police to ban "beggars night" and to fine and jail parents "who put their children out to beg." In 1966 a Bay Ridge, Brooklyn parish started a Ragamuffin Parade for kids "dressed in their parents' oversized clothing." It's still held every fall, in October.

**Recommended chain** (six beats, ~60 s; beat 2's mask line or beat 4's Macy's line are the cuts if long)
1. ★ **The question, and the answer in one line: they were copying the grown-ups.** Around 1870, a pastor said, New York's costume clubs paraded on Thanksgiving, and the kids imitated them in old clothes "many sizes too large." (The oversized coat from 10's last frame *is* the answer: open on it.)
2. **The grown-ups quit; the kids kept going, and started asking.** "Anything for Thanksgiving?" Pennies, apples, candy, on street corners and at doors. *(Optional color: before Halloween, Thanksgiving was when mask-makers did their biggest business.)*
3. ★ **Then the adults turned on it.** A 1903 editorial: ringing every doorbell for handouts was "long past a joke." In 1922 police were told to stop the begging; in 1930 the schools chief called it out of step with modern life. (Pick two, not all three.)
4. ★ **The fix: a parade with a slogan.** A boys' club marched kids in costume under the words "American boys do not beg." By about 1940 the begging was gone. *(Optional: the parades faded in the 1950s, overshadowed by Macy's.)*
5. ★ **The twist: right then, a new custom reached New York from the West.** Kids in costume, ringing doorbells, asking for treats, on Halloween. And some adults complained it was begging, all over again. (Hedge: say it came from elsewhere; don't say Ragamuffin Day turned into it.)
6. ★ **Last beat: the costume survived, a month early.** In Brooklyn, kids still march in their parents' too-big clothes in the Ragamuffin Parade, every October. Then the tease (Macy's balloons, see Tease).

Mechanism stays at about two sentences (beat 1). The conflict (beats 3–5) carries the middle, per the analytics note.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"They were copying the grown-ups,"** framed as what people said at the time ("a pastor explained in 1909," "papers at the time said"). Nobody knows the deeper origin (Sun 1915).
- **Start:** "by the 1880s" or "around 1870, a pastor said." Not "began in 1870."
- **Door to door:** "at doors and on street corners." The 1903 editorial supports the doorbells.
- **Police 1922:** "police were told to discourage it" / "to stop the begging." Not "banned Ragamuffin Day" (the Herald says fancy dress and parading were still allowed).
- **Slogan:** "American boys do not beg" (exact). No year, or "by the early 1940s." Not "don't," not "1937."
- **End:** "by about 1940 the begging was gone." Parades "faded in the 1950s." Not "ended in 1956."
- **Macy's:** "took over" / "overshadowed." Never "grew out of," "replaced on purpose," or "was inspired by."
- **Trick-or-treating:** "a new custom reached New York from out West" / "came from elsewhere." Never "Ragamuffin Day became trick-or-treating," "moved to Halloween," or "the origin of trick-or-treating." "Looked a lot like it" is fine.
- **Bay Ridge:** "in Brooklyn, every fall (in October)." Not "on Halloween."
- **"Beg":** fine to say. The kids called it a game and the adults called it begging; that's the conflict.
- **No question pivot** more than once; no "chain."

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Ragamuffin Day is the origin of trick-or-treating / it moved to Halloween."** Kawash: no evidence; trick-or-treat came from the West and reached the East in the late 1940s. This is the main groove (Atlas Obscura: "the roots of modern-day Halloween lie in this forgotten American tradition"; Mental Floss: "trick-or-treating moved to Halloween").
2. **"Kids dressed as beggars because they were poor."** It was a costume ("old clothes, many sizes too large"), worn by children across the city; the begging was a game that adults came to see as real begging, especially in the Depression.
3. **"The Macy's parade grew out of the ragamuffins."** No source. Macy's began as a store's Christmas parade (1924).
4. **"American boys don't beg (1937)."** It's "do not," and 1937 is when the counter-parade began.
5. **"Ragamuffin Day ended in 1956."** That's the last newspaper mention of the parades; the begging was gone by about 1940.
6. **"It started in 1870."** One pastor's estimate, 40 years later. Documented by the 1880s.
7. **"It came from Irish immigrants / Guy Fawkes / St. Martin's Day."** Atlas Obscura says "thought to have begun in Irish and other immigrant neighborhoods"; 6sqft floats saints' days. Nobody shows it. The 1915 Sun: origin "beyond his power of discovery."
8. **"Thanksgiving used to be like Halloween with tricks."** Thanksgiving kids asked; they didn't threaten pranks the way 1930s trick-or-treaters did (the Sun 1915: the Halloween-style pranks "seem not to be essential to the ragamuffin's pleasure"). Good contrast with Short 10; don't blur them.

## Bridge verdict (from 10)

**10's last scene (narration v3.1):** "Kids once got treats to stop a real threat. Now the treats come sealed, against a danger that was mostly a myth." Then: "And why did New York kids once go door to door in costume on Thanksgiving? That's another story." Drawing: a kid in an oversized coat and hat holding out a hat at a door with a paper turkey; the paint lands on the turkey (word "Thanksgiving").

**Does the bridge hold?**
- **Real, the shared thing: a costumed kid at the door, asking.** 1903 NYT (doorbells), Tribune 1908 ("levying tribute on stores and homes"), NYG&B, NPR 2025 ("begging door to door"). Holds.
- **Real, the shared idea: adults deciding whether a kid at the door is playing or begging.** 10's kids threatened; 11's kids asked, and adults still fought it. A good contrast with 10, not a repeat.
- **Tuleja's "missing link" (via Kawash) is real as a scholar's suggestion,** and Kawash rejects the cause: "no evidence that the actual practice of trick-or-treating originated in New York." So the bridge is a resemblance, not descent. 11 must not claim descent.
- **The planted drawing is accurate:** "old clothes, many sizes too large" (Farrar 1909) matches 10's oversized coat and hat exactly. Bay Ridge 1966 matches it too ("parents' oversized clothing"). Open 11 on that drawing: the paint jumps from the turkey to the coat sleeve.

**Verdict:** the tease is true and the bridge holds as a shared thing and a shared idea. No change to 10 needed.

## Groove risk
- **What generic AI Shorts say:** "Before Halloween, there was Ragamuffin Day! In the 1870s, New York kids dressed as beggars on Thanksgiving and asked 'Anything for Thanksgiving?' It faded in the 1930s, the Macy's parade replaced it, and trick-or-treating moved to Halloween." Often with "this is where trick-or-treating comes from." Every Ragamuffin listicle and Short says this (Vintage News, Atlas Obscura, Mental Floss, IrishCentral).
- **How we differ:** answer the "why" no one answers: the kids were copying grown men's costume parades, and the grown-ups quit first (1907, 1909 papers). Then the fight (police order, school circular, "American boys do not beg"), then the twist that the same doorbell ask came back on Halloween from out West and got called begging again, then the October parade in Brooklyn where kids still wear their parents' clothes. **No "origin of trick-or-treating," no "Macy's replaced it" as a cause.**
- **Visual groove to avoid:** Pilgrims, cornucopias, cartoon turkeys, Dickens street urchins with soot faces, sepia "old photo" frames.

## Tease for Short 12 (thread rule "two ahead")

**Candidate A (recommended): "Why does the Macy's parade have giant balloons?"**
- **Real link (shared thing):** the Thanksgiving costume parade in New York's streets. 11 names the Macy's parade as the one that took over from the ragamuffin parades (MCNY 2019; NPR 2025; NYPL 2010). Both are about kids on Thanksgiving streets.
- **Chain (verified, three sources):** the 1924 parade had live Central Park Zoo animals; they frightened children close up (MCNY 2019; America Comes Alive 2016); by 1927 puppeteer Tony Sarg was asked to "entertain rather than terrify the crowds" and turned to balloons with Goodyear (Hyperallergic, Allison Meier, Nov. 23, 2016). Felix the Cat, 1927. Then the far end: from 1928 the balloons were **let go at the end of the parade, with a reward for whoever returned them**; in 1932 a student pilot flew into one ("I think I'll have a piece of the neck") and her instructor pulled the plane out of a dive near the ground. That ended the releases (MCNY; Hyperallergic; America Comes Alive, citing NYT Nov. 25, 1932, "Fliers Hit Balloon; Plunge 5000 Feet").
- **Hedges:** "frightened some children" (America Comes Alive says some; MCNY says the animals frightened them). The pilot's name differs (Annette Gipson / Ann Gibson); don't name her. The reward was $100 in 1928 (America Comes Alive only).
- **Groove note:** "the first parade had zoo animals" is common listicle trivia. The far end (released balloons, a reward, a plane in a nosedive) is the fresh part. Run the groove check before scripting.
- **Tease line:** "And why does the Macy's parade have giant balloons? That's another story."
- **Plant:** in 11's last scene (Bay Ridge kids marching), a balloon's round shape peeking over the rooftops at the far end of the street. Or in the Macy's line, the parade drawn with one balloon above it.

**Candidate B (backup): "Why did America once have two Thanksgivings?"**
- **Real link:** the Macy's parade began as a store's *Christmas* parade, with Santa at the end opening the shopping season (NYT 1924 via America Comes Alive). In 1939, worried about a short Christmas shopping season, Roosevelt moved Thanksgiving a week earlier; states split (23 + D.C. vs 22, three both, in 1939), and Congress fixed the fourth Thursday in a resolution signed Dec. 26, 1941 ([National Archives, Center for Legislative Archives](https://www.archives.gov/legislative/features/thanksgiving); [Newspapers.com blog, "FDR Moves Thanksgiving" (2015)](https://blog.newspapers.com/fdr-moves-thanksgiving/) ("retail lobbyists worried"); [Wikipedia, "Franksgiving"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franksgiving) (Fred Lazarus Jr. of Federated Department Stores urged it)). Shared thing: Santa at the end of the parade, and the shopping season it opened. Plant Santa on the last float.
- **Caution:** don't say Macy's lobbied for it (Federated didn't own Macy's until 1994). "Franksgiving" is well-known trivia: the groove check needs a far end (e.g., football schedules, the "Republican Thanksgiving") before it's used. Better saved for a mid-November slot.

**Not recommended:** the paper turkey on 10's door (no documented history found tying it to ragamuffins); "Why do we eat turkey on Thanksgiving?" (no real bridge from 11; save for late November).
