# Short 13 research: "Why do Americans vote on a Tuesday?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-26 for a post around Nov 1-2 (Election Day is Tue Nov 3, 2026, a midterm). Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** "Why do Americans vote on a Tuesday?" Politically neutral by construction: it is about a 181-year-old calendar rule, and the sources are the statute and the floor debate.
**Verdict:** **Go, with the story told against the usual answer.** The usual answer ("farmers needed a day to buggy to the county seat, Monday would mean Sunday travel, Wednesday was market day, and the 'first Monday' wording dodges All Saints' Day and merchants' month-end books") is **not in the congressional record.** I read the House and Senate debates on the 1845 law (Congressional Globe, Dec 1844 to Jan 1845, plus the 1844 session that failed first). **Nobody gives a reason for Tuesday.** One member, who helped write the wording, said "as to the particular day, he regarded it as of no importance" (Elmer, Dec 9, 1844, p. 14, read from the page image). What the record does say: (1) states had been voting on their own days across a 34-day window set in 1792; (2) the stated purpose was to stop fraud, meaning people being moved from state to state to vote more than once, and both parties had just accused each other of it; (3) the odd phrase "the Tuesday next after the first Monday" exists because a plain "first Tuesday in November" would sometimes land on Nov 1, which is 36 days before the electors meet, outside the 34-day window. **The far-end surprise:** "after the first Monday" is arithmetic, not religion or merchants. It makes the election land exactly 29 days before the electors' Wednesday every single time (our arithmetic; checked by script for 1848-2100). The buggy/Sabbath/market-day story is plausible and widely told, but the earliest sources I can find for it are NPR 2012, an encyclopedia entry (2014) and a book (2016); the All Saints/merchants half goes back at least to an FEC FAQ quoted in 2004. None cites an 1840s source. **Say "the story you've heard", never "Congress reasoned".** Everything below is rated; what I could not verify is at the bottom.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

Until 1845 the Constitution left the election day to Congress and Congress had never picked one. A 1792 law only said each state must choose its presidential electors "within thirty-four days preceding the first Wednesday in December." So states voted whenever they liked inside a month-long window. In 1840 the first votes came Oct 30; in 1844, Nov 1, with the electors meeting Dec 4. After the 1844 election both parties were accusing each other of fraud ("both parties are charging each other with having committed great frauds, and both professed to be anxious to guard against them in future," Rep. Haralson, Dec 13, 1844). The worry was voters being moved from one state to another, and voting twice, because the days differed. Rep. Alexander Duncan of Ohio (Cincinnati) introduced a "uniform time" bill in Jan 1844; it passed the House but stalled in the Senate (June 17, 1844, a Senate fight about delaying it until after that year's election). Duncan brought it back on Dec 4, 1844; the House voted 179-0 to suspend the rules so he could introduce it, and passed it 187-1 on Dec 16. The Senate passed it in one sitting, "without any debate," on Jan 16, 1845. President Tyler signed it Jan 23, 1845. First uniform election: Nov 7, 1848.

The wording is the surprise. Duncan's draft said "the first Tuesday in the month of November." He then moved to change it to "the first Tuesday after the first Monday" because "the first Tuesday of November might, in some cases, be more than thirty days from the first Wednesday in December" (p. 14). In the Senate, Berrien of Georgia said the same thing in the same terms and offered the final phrase: "a case might arise in which the first Tuesday in November might not be within the thirty-four days prior to the first Wednesday in December ... it would be still better to say 'the Tuesday next after the first Monday in November'" (Jan 16, 1845, p. 143). If November 1 is a Tuesday (1864 was one; on today's calendar so would 2016 have been), the first Wednesday in December is 36 days later. (The 34-day rule itself ended with later changes to the electors' meeting date, so this is how the 1845 wording worked, not how today's calendar works.) The fix makes the gap exactly 29 days every time.

Why November and not October or December was argued, and the arguments are weather and logistics, not harvest: a member tried to move it to October (Duncan: it would break the 34-day law; "the public sentiment had appointed this time"); a South Carolina member wanted the first Monday in December, and was answered that December is "very cold" and that Congress itself convenes that day ("the worst day of all others"). A northern senator asked for the second Tuesday because some states' legislatures met in late October; Tappan said any day would inconvenience some state, and the first Tuesday after the first Monday won. A small comic detail: the bill as the House passed it **left out the month**; the Senate Judiciary Committee caught it ("No month was mentioned in the bill"), and Duncan said he supposed the omission "occurred in engrossing it" (a clerk's slip, our reading; his words are OCR-read).

Congress later copied the day: House elections from 1876 (Act of Feb 2, 1872, 17 Stat. 28, sec. 3) and Senate elections from 1914 (CRS). **A midterm Nov 3, 2026 is therefore on the 1872 rule, not the 1845 rule.** The 1845 law covers presidential electors only. Today 47 states and DC offer early in-person voting (NCSL via CRS, as of April 2026), so Tuesday is the last day to vote in most places, not the only one.

**Recommended chain** (six beats, ~70 s; beat 6 is the cut if long)
1. ★ **Hook, the groove broken in the first line.** "The story is buggies, Sundays and market day. I read the debate. Nobody says it." Then: before 1845 every state picked its own day. Hedge: "in the House and Senate debate I could read" (see Hedges).
2. ★ **The problem: a month of voting.** A 1792 law gave states a 34-day window. In 1844 the first state voted Nov 1; the electors met Dec 4. Lawmakers feared people being moved from state to state to vote again, and both parties had just accused each other of it. (Use "feared"; don't claim it happened.)
3. ★ **The fix and the speed.** An Ohio congressman, Alexander Duncan, wrote a bill for one day, everywhere. The House passed it 187 to 1. The Senate passed it in one sitting, no debate. Signed Jan 23, 1845.
4. ★ **The weird phrase.** His draft said "first Tuesday in November." In some years that's Nov 1, which is 36 days before the electors meet, outside the 34-day rule. So: "the Tuesday after the first Monday." That wording makes the gap 29 days, every time. (The "first Monday" is math. Not the Sabbath, not All Saints.)
5. ★ **The twist: nobody said why Tuesday.** One member who wrote the wording said the day itself was "of no importance." The buggy and market-day reasons are explanations people came up with later; I couldn't find them in the 1840s record. (Optional color: the bill nearly left November out; a clerk's slip.)
6. **Last beat, neutral and short.** Congress copied the day for the House (1872) and Senate (1914), so Tuesday Nov 3 is on a rule older than the telephone. Today 47 states and DC allow early voting, so it's the last day more than the only one. End on the date; no "go vote," no side.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"In the House and Senate debate I could read" / "in the debate, nobody says."** Not "Congress never considered farmers." The Globe is a summary, a committee report (Feb 1844) wasn't found, and an unrecorded reason is possible.
- **Fraud:** "lawmakers feared people were being moved from state to state to vote again, and both parties were accusing each other." Never "fraud happened in 1840," never name a party.
- **The wording:** "a plain first Tuesday in November could land on Nov 1, 36 days before the electors met, past the 34-day limit." "After the first Monday" fixes it to 29 days. Say "the debate says" or "Congress said at the time" for the 34-day reason; that is on the record, twice.
- **1845 vs 2026:** the 1845 law covers the presidential election. This Nov 3 is a midterm, on the 1872 (House) and 1914 (Senate) rules.
- **Count of states:** "most states offer early voting" unless the CRS number is re-checked.
- **Votes:** "187 to 1" (House), "no debate" (Senate), both from the Globe. "179 to 0" is the vote to suspend the rules and introduce it.

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Farmers needed a day to get to the county seat, so not Monday (Sunday travel) and not Wednesday (market day)."** Plausible, widely told, no 1840s source found. The record's reason for the wording is the 34-day window; for the law, fraud and uniformity.
2. **"The 'after the first Monday' wording keeps the vote off All Saints' Day / merchants' month-end books."** FEC FAQ and an encyclopedia entry say so; the Globe says arithmetic. The effect (never Nov 1) is the same; the reason is not documented.
3. **"Election Day is in November because of harvest and good roads."** CRS (Orr 2016) says so; the floor debate only has weather against December and logistics against October.
4. **"The telegraph forced one day"** (Green Papers). No support in the debates.
5. **"The early states' results were swaying the late states."** Modern framing (Ackerman via Time). The 1844-45 worry was repeat voting.
6. **"1845 law set Election Day for everything."** Presidential electors only. House 1872/1876; Senate 1914.
7. **"The Constitution requires a Tuesday."** It says only that Congress sets the day and it must be the same everywhere (Art. II, sec. 1).
8. **"Elections lasted a month / until Dec 4, 1844"** as a flat statement. First votes Nov 1; electors met Dec 4.

## Groove risk
- **What generic AI Shorts say:** "In 1845 Congress picked Tuesday because farmers needed a day to travel, Sunday was church, Wednesday was market day." Every explainer, plus Wikipedia-style trivia. Add "and Monday was out because of Sunday travel," often with "All Saints" as a bonus.
- **How we differ:** we open by saying the usual answer isn't in the debate, then give what is: the 34-day window, the fraud fear, the 29-day arithmetic, "of no importance," the missing month. The audience gets a surprise (the wording is math) and an honest hedge (nobody said why Tuesday).
- **Visual groove to avoid:** buggies, ballot boxes with flags, voting-booth clip art, red/blue anything, stars and stripes. (Brand paint is red-lead #D9431E; keep it off any "red state" reading, so put it on the calendar and on the two numbers, not on party colors.)
