# Short 05 research: "Why was margarine once dyed pink?"

**Status:** Deep-verified 2026-10-07. This is research only, with no script yet.
**Candidate:** not in the story bank. Chosen as the link out of 04 (color as the line between real and imitation food, and governments fighting fakes with color rules).
**Verdict:** strong, if pink is the door and not the story. The pink laws, the dye capsule and Wisconsin's restaurant rule are the groove: 99% Invisible (ep. 462, 2021), Smithsonian, National Geographic, Zócalo, JSTOR Daily and a dozen Wisconsin outlets all tell them. Our edge is the far end, and it's a good one: **margarine was dyed yellow from its very first patent because butter was dyed first.** The first federal law about margarine (1886) defined butter as butter "with or without additional coloring matter"; in 1904 the Supreme Court upheld a 40-to-1 tax on a dealer whose margarine was yellow only because the *butter* in his recipe had been dyed; and the 1938 federal food law, still in force, exempts butter (with cheese and ice cream) from having to declare artificial color on the label. Two more primary-law oddities almost nobody tells: since 1931 the legal line between "yellow" and "not yellow" margarine has been **1.6 degrees of yellow on a Lovibond tintometer**, a gauge a Victorian brewer built to grade beer; and since 1950 federal law has said unlabeled margarine served in a restaurant must be cut **"triangular in shape"**. Both are in the U.S. Code today (checked 2026-10-07).

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

Margarine has no yellow of its own. Made from beef fat in 1869 and from vegetable oils later, it comes out pale, nearly white, and its name comes from a Greek word for pearl. But it was never meant to stay white: the inventor's own US patent (1873) says to add "the necessary quantity of yellow color, which is employed for the ordinary butter". Butter was the dyed one first. Cows on winter hay give pale butter, so dairies had long tinted it with annatto to look like June, and when Congress passed its first margarine law in 1886 (a 2-cent tax and licenses, enforced with revenue stamps like tobacco), it defined butter as butter "with or without additional coloring matter" and left margarine's yellow alone. Dairy states went after the color. New Hampshire ordered margarine dyed pink in 1885; Vermont, Minnesota, West Virginia and South Dakota followed. In 1889 a New Hampshire jury watched the state's chemist dye a sample pink in court with cochineal, after the makers swore it couldn't be done. In 1891 Wheeling merchants ordered the stuff from Chicago "duly colored pink", and "good housewives said [it was] uneatable"; two dealers were fined $20 for selling it white. In 1898 the Supreme Court threw the pink law out: forcing a color nobody will buy is a ban in disguise, and "a legislature could not be limited to pink in its choice of colors... it might equally as well provide that it should be colored blue or red or black." So Congress taxed the color instead. From 1902, yellow margarine paid 10 cents a pound and white paid a quarter of a cent, forty times less, and the law let families color it "at his own family table". For most of the next fifty years margarine came white with a bead of yellow dye to knead in at the kitchen table. In 1904 the Court upheld the 10-cent tax on a dealer whose margarine was yellow only because the butter in the recipe had been dyed with a commercial "butter color" made in Burlington, Vermont, a pink-law state. In 1931 Congress wrote the line as a number: more than 1.6 degrees of yellow on a Lovibond tintometer, the brewer's beer-color gauge. In 1950 it ended the taxes but kept the number and added a shape: a restaurant serving colored margarine without a label on each pat must cut it "triangular in shape". Wisconsin held out until 1967, and its statute still makes serving margarine instead of butter, unasked, an offense with jail time on paper. And the real thing? Under the same federal food law, since 1938, butter, cheese and ice cream don't have to say they're dyed.

**Recommended chain** (eight beats; cut 6 or 7 if the read runs long)
1. Margarine is naturally pale (the name means pearl), and it was dyed yellow from the first patent (1873): "the yellow color which is employed for the ordinary butter".
2. Butter was dyed first: pale winter butter, annatto. The first federal margarine law (1886) defined butter "with or without additional coloring matter".
3. ★ Dairy states ordered margarine pink (New Hampshire 1885, then four more). What was actually shipped pink (Wheeling, 1891) nobody would eat.
4. ★ 1898: the Supreme Court struck the pink law down. A color nobody will buy is a ban; "it might equally as well provide that it should be colored blue or red or black."
5. ★ So Congress taxed the color: 1902, 10 cents a pound for yellow, a quarter cent for white. Margarine went white, with a dye bead kneaded in at home for about fifty years (Wisconsin: until 1967).
6. 1904: a dealer was taxed the full 10 cents because the butter in his margarine had been dyed. Still "artificially colored", said the Court.
7. 1931: the line became a number, 1.6 degrees of yellow on a brewer's beer gauge. 1950: the taxes ended, the number stayed, and unlabeled restaurant margarine had to be triangles. Both still in the U.S. Code.
8. Far end: butter is still tinted, and the 1938 federal food law still says butter, cheese and ice cream don't have to declare it.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"Pale, nearly white"** for margarine's natural color, not "pure white". Beef-fat margarine and vegetable-oil margarine are both pale; the 1904 pleading says "naturally white", and that's a litigant talking.
- **"From a Greek word for pearl"**, not "margarine means pearl". The pearl is in margaric acid's name.
- **"New Hampshire first, in 1885, then a handful of states"** for the pink laws. Vermont is 1884 or 1886 depending on the source; Minnesota and West Virginia 1891; South Dakota 1897 (USDA) or 1891 (Hisano). Don't list years in the script.
- **"The little that was actually shipped pink, nobody would eat"** (Wheeling, 1891). Never "pink margarine was sold for years" or "Americans ate pink margarine".
- **"The Supreme Court struck New Hampshire's pink law down"**, not "banned pink laws". The ground was interstate commerce.
- **"Forty times"** is exact (10 cents vs a quarter cent). "A few makers' licenses cost $600" is fine; don't mix the license fees into the per-pound line.
- **"For most of the next fifty years"** for the dye capsule (1902–1950 federally), and "in Wisconsin until 1967". Don't claim every household kneaded; the capsule era is best documented from the 1930s–1950s, with Jelke's dye earlier.
- **"Some butter is still tinted"**, usually with annatto. Not "all butter is dyed".
- **"Doesn't have to say so"** for the 1938 exemption. Certified dyes must still be declared, and many brands list annatto anyway.
- **"Still in the U.S. Code" / "still on the books"** for the triangle rule and Wisconsin's law. Neither is enforced in practice (Wisconsin's agriculture department says warning letters, one or two complaints a year).
- **"A Victorian brewer's gauge"** for Lovibond. The Tintometer was built for beer and then used across industry.
- **"In 1950 Congress ended the taxes"**: signed March 16, 1950, in force July 1, 1950. Not 1951.
- **"A dealer"** for McCray; I didn't verify his city. The maker was the Ohio Butterine Company (don't name it).
- **"As a margarine dealer told the Supreme Court"** if using "substantially all butter... was colored artificially". It's a pleading accepted as true for the appeal, not a finding.
- **No health claims** about margarine, butter, annatto or beta-carotene. Trans fats are a different story and out of scope.

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Margarine is one molecule away from plastic", "it was invented to fatten turkeys and killed them", "it shares 27 ingredients with paint", "flies won't land on it."** A chain email, all false ([Science-Based Medicine](https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/fake-news-about-margarine/); [Lead Stories](https://leadstories.com/hoax-alert/2019/05/fake-news-margarine-is-not-one-molecule-away-from-plastic.html)). The real origin is the 1869 French prize ([AOCS](https://www.aocs.org/resource/hippolyte-mege-1817-1880/)).
2. **"Some states required margarine to be red, brown or black."** No state did. The USDA's 1939 compilation lists only pink laws. "Blue or red or black" is the Supreme Court's hypothetical in Collins, and National Geographic says other colors were "proposed". Keep it to pink.
3. **"Margarine was never actually dyed pink."** It was, at least in Wheeling in 1891 (two papers), and a chemist did it in a New Hampshire courtroom in 1889. It just didn't sell.
4. **"Margarine was white until the 1950s" / "the government banned yellow margarine."** Federal law never banned yellow; it taxed it 40-to-1 (1902) and margarine was dyed yellow from the first patent. About thirty states did ban yellow, and Wisconsin's ban lasted to 1967.
5. **"The federal tax was repealed in 1951."** March 16, 1950, effective July 1, 1950 ([21 U.S.C. 347 note](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/347)).
6. **"Wisconsin banned margarine."** It banned *colored* margarine (1895–1967) and taxed the white kind (15 cents a pound in 1965).
7. **"A senator's failed blindfold taste test ended Wisconsin's ban."** The 1965 test happened (AP), but National Geographic dates it 1955, the "his wife fed him margarine" coda is family lore, and the repeal came two years later for economic reasons. The groove answer for Wisconsin; skip it.
8. **"You can be arrested in Wisconsin for serving margarine."** The statute carries jail on paper; the state says it's never enforced.
9. **"Napoleon ordered margarine for his army."** Napoleon III's government offered a prize; Mège won it.
10. **"The Supreme Court said states can't regulate margarine's color."** It upheld a state ban on yellow margarine in 1894 (Plumley) and struck down compulsory pink in 1898 (Collins). The rule is: you may stop it looking like butter; you may not force it to look disgusting.
11. **"Margarine is legally required to be yellow today."** Color is optional in the federal standard (21 CFR 166.110).

## Bridge from Short 04

**Short 04's last scene (as researched):** the 1940 FDA definition ("dyed red") and Red No. 3's end date, January 15, 2027. The thread note for 04 says the bridges out could run through "dye and color, cherries, Oregon, food rules or jars".

**Is there a real shared thing?** Yes, a shared rule-idea and a shared material, but not a shared cause.
- **Real, the idea:** both stories are the government drawing the line between real and imitation food with color. In 04, a dyed fake took the name: "maraschino" became cherries "dyed red" (1940). In 05, color was the weapon against the fake: pink by law (1885), a 40-to-1 tax on yellow (1902), a number on a brewer's gauge (1931), a triangle (1950). The twist that ties them: the real thing in 05 was dyed too, and the law lets it keep quiet (1938).
- **Real, the material:** cochineal. Short 04's 1911 seizure records list "Maraschino Cherries — Colored with Cochineal Lake" and "Whole Cochineal Maraschino Cherries"; in 1889 the New Hampshire state chemist dyed margarine pink in court "using a preparation of cochineal" ([People and Patriot, 1889](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn84020381/1889-04-25/ed-1/?sp=1)). One period source for the margarine side, so use it as a visual (the same red insect dye tints a cherry, then a pat) rather than a narrated claim, or narrate it with "as one paper reported".
- **Real, the word:** "imitation". 04's 1912 rule required "Imitation Maraschino Cherries"; the 1902 margarine act is titled "An Act to make oleomargarine and other imitation dairy products subject to the laws of any State..." and the 1886 act is "An Act defining butter". A real rule-word, not a pun.
- **Real, the statute:** the 1950 margarine rules were written into the same Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (1938) under which the FDA ruled on maraschino cherries in 1940. Too abstract to narrate; fine for the description.
- **Not real:** no person, company or dye links the two. I found no record that erythrosine (Red 3) was used in margarine or butter, and no record that cherry makers and margarine makers overlapped. Don't imply a chain from cherries to margarine.
- **Not real (don't reach for it):** the 1907 federal dye list, Wiley or the Bureau of Chemistry. The 1886 and 1902 margarine laws were tax laws run by Internal Revenue, not food-purity laws, and 04 keeps Wiley unnamed anyway.

**A tease that stays true.** 04 ends on a cherry that the law defines by its dye, and a dye that the law is now removing. Suggested last line for 04: **"And why did the law once want margarine dyed pink? That's another story."** On screen: the jar of cherries beside a pale block of margarine; a drop of the cherry's red paint lands on the block and turns it pink. Two honest alternatives: **"And why was margarine once dyed pink? That's another story."** (true in the Wheeling sense), or, if 04 ends on the "dyed red" definition rather than Red 3: **"And what happens when the law wants the fake to be a color nobody will buy? That's another story."** (longer, but it names the shared idea).
