# Short 07 research: "Why does Swiss cheese have fewer holes now?"

**Status:** Deep-verified 2026-10-09, before Short 06's voice. Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** story bank #19 ("specks of hay, and spotless modern milking removed them"), chosen as the link out of 06 (winter hay makes pale butter in 06; hay specks make the holes in 07; 06's last line names cheese).
**Verdict:** safe to tease exactly as written, and better than the bank entry promised. The bank's whole chain (bacteria make the gas, hay specks seed the holes, clean milking removed the specks, Agroscope 2015, makers can add hay dust) is the groove: BBC, ITV, the Boston Globe, Discover, Fox and a dozen Shorts told it in 2015, and most AI retellings end at "so they add hay dust now." Our edge is past that. **In Switzerland the makers had to go to court for the hay.** The Swiss government's agriculture office refused to let hay-flower powder into the Emmentaler AOP rulebook (Nov. 15, 2023) because it "is not a traditional production method"; the Federal Administrative Court overturned it on April 2, 2025, holding that the powder "is not a traditional feature, but restores an original, traditional feature of Emmentaler," ordered the office to write it in, and made the office pay the cheesemakers CHF 12,000. The powder went into the official specification on Nov. 4, 2025, and a cheesemaker now uses "the tip of a knife" of ground hay flowers for 600 kg of cheese. **In America the holes shrank on purpose.** The USDA's top grade used to require most holes to be 11/16 to 13/16 inch (the small end is about a dime), and in 2001 it dropped the minimum to 3/8 inch because big-holed cheese "does not perform well on modern slicing equipment"; Switzerland's agriculture office wrote in to object. Put the two rulebooks side by side and they barely touch: the Swiss rule wants holes "mostly 2 to 4 cm"; the American top grade tops out at 13/16 inch, which is 2.06 cm. Both are primary and checked 2026-10-09.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

The holes in Swiss cheese are gas. Bacteria added to the milk eat the lactic acid in the young cheese and give off carbon dioxide; in an 80-kg wheel, around 120 litres of it. But gas doesn't make a hole by itself. It needs a starting point, a speck to collect on, the way a bubble in a glass forms on a scratch. For a century nobody knew what the specks were. In 1917 an American dairy chemist wrote in the brand-new Journal of Dairy Science that the holes looked "seeded", like crystals, and that the gas "separates at advantageous points". In 2015 the Swiss federal dairy lab, Agroscope, found the seeds: microscopic specks of hay. Hay is hollow, so each speck carries a tiny bubble of air into the milk, and the bacteria's gas blows that bubble up into a hole. Five to ten milligrams of hay particles in a tonne of milk is enough. Add more, and the lab could set the number of holes "almost at will". For generations the specks fell into open milking pails in the barn. From about 2005 Swiss farms switched to closed pipelines, milking robots and cooling tanks, the milk got cleaner than it had ever been, and Emmentaler wheels started coming out short of holes. (Cheese with none is "blind", in the USDA's own glossary.) So in July 2022 the Emmentaler makers asked the Federal Office for Agriculture to let them add ground hay flowers to the vat. The office said no: not traditional; the holes are fine; filter the milk less instead. The makers appealed, calling the policy "romanticising and ideological, detached from scientific foundations." On April 2, 2025 the Federal Administrative Court ruled for the cheese: hay powder "is not a traditional feature, but restores an original, traditional feature," there is no risk of standardising the cheese "since there can also be too many holes," and the office owed the makers CHF 12,000. The powder is in the official rulebook as of Nov. 4, 2025. Meanwhile, the American rulebook went the other way. Since 2001 the USDA's top grade for Swiss cheese has accepted holes as small as 3/8 inch, down from 11/16, about a dime, because the old holes "do not perform well on modern slicing equipment" and "consumer preference points toward a smaller eye." Switzerland's agriculture office wrote to Washington to object. It lost that one too. So the smallest hole the Swiss rule wants, 2 cm, is about the biggest hole America's top grade allows.

**Recommended chain** (eight beats; cut 3 or 6 if the read runs long)
1. The holes are gas from bacteria eating the cheese's acid: 120 litres in a big wheel. But gas needs a speck to start a bubble on.
2. ★ Agroscope, 2015: the specks are hay. Hay is hollow, each speck carries a bubble of air, and the gas blows it up. A pinch of hay dust in a tonne of milk sets the number of holes "almost at will."
3. 1917: an American chemist had guessed the holes were "seeded", like crystals. It took 98 years to find the seed. (Optional.)
4. ★ The specks used to fall into open pails in the barn. From about 2005, closed pipelines, milking robots and cooling tanks made Swiss milk the cleanest it had ever been, and the holes started to go. Cheese with none is "blind."
5. ★ 2022: the makers asked to add ground hay flowers. The government's agriculture office said no: not traditional. Filter the milk less instead.
6. 2025: the Federal Administrative Court ruled for the cheese. The hay "isn't traditional, but it restores a traditional feature." The office paid the makers' costs. A cheesemaker now uses the tip of a knife for 600 kilos of cheese.
7. Far end A: in America the holes shrank on purpose. In 2001 the USDA cut the smallest top-grade hole from about a dime to 3/8 inch, because the big ones tear in slicing machines. Switzerland wrote to object.
8. Far end B (last line before the tease): the Swiss rule says holes mostly 2 to 4 cm. America's top grade stops at 2. The smallest Swiss hole is the biggest American one.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"Bacteria added to the milk"** make the gas; hay only decides **where and how many** holes. Never "the holes come from hay" without the gas, and never "dirt" (Agroscope rejected the word).
- **"Specks of hay" / "hay dust"**, and "each speck carries a tiny bubble of air" (the 2026 capillary result). Not "hay seeds."
- **"From about 2005"** or **"over the last twenty years"** for the decline (court; ZHAW). Agroscope in 2015 said "10–15 years." Don't say "since the 1990s."
- **"The holes started to go"** / "too few holes," not "disappeared." The office's point in court was that quality stayed high; the court's point was that cheese short of holes couldn't be sold as Emmentaler, so it never showed in the grading figures.
- **"A Swiss federal court"** or "the Federal Administrative Court." Not "the Supreme Court." Judgment Apr. 2, 2025; spec amended Nov. 4, 2025.
- **"Paid the cheesemakers' costs"** (CHF 12,000 party costs). Not "fined."
- **"The USDA's top grade"**: a voluntary grade standard, not a law. "Since 2001" (effective Feb. 22, 2001). Not FDA.
- **"About a dime"** for 11/16 inch; give **3/8 inch** as the new minimum; 13/16 inch is the top (2.06 cm). If the last line compares rulebooks, say "about 2 cm" for both ends; don't say "exactly."
- **"Slicing machines"** is fair (USDA's own words to swissinfo; CSM; NPR). The Federal Register says "automated portioning and packaging equipment."
- **"Switzerland's agriculture office wrote in to object"** (one comment in the record). Not "Switzerland protested" as a diplomatic act.
- **"Swiss cheese"** in the US sense vs **"Emmentaler"** in Switzerland: say "In Switzerland, the real thing is Emmentaler" once, then "the cheese."
- **No "mystery solved" framing**: Clark proposed a nucleus in 1917; Agroscope identified it in 2015 and confirmed the mechanism in 2026.
- **"The tip of a knife for 600 kilos"** is one maker's dose (2026). Agroscope's lab figure is 5–10 mg per tonne of milk.

## Myths to avoid
1. **"The holes are made by bacteria, full stop."** True of the gas, but the 2015 result is that the specks decide the holes. Most AI Shorts stop at bacteria (and name "P. shermani," an older name).
2. **"The holes come from dirt in the milk."** The BBC headline. Agroscope's supplement calls it "a false oversimplification." Say hay.
3. **"Scientists finally solved the mystery in 2015."** An American proposed the nucleus idea in 1917; Agroscope found the nucleus (2015) and confirmed the air-bubble mechanism (2026).
4. **"The FDA changed the hole size."** It was the USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service, a voluntary grade standard (Mental Floss says FDA).
5. **"Grade A holes must be 3/16 to 1 3/8 inch"** (LiveScience) or **"3/8 to 11/16"** (CEI blog). The standard says 3/8 to 13/16 inch.
6. **"Big-holed cheese is illegal in America."** Grades are voluntary; big holes just fall outside Grade A/B ("slight large eyed": over 13/16 inch).
7. **"Switzerland banned hay powder."** The BLW refused to add it to the AOP specification; hay powder was already used by German and French Emmental makers (The Local, SRF). The court ordered it in.
8. **"The Swiss Supreme Court ruled."** Federal Administrative Court. The Supreme Court (Bundesgericht) didn't hear it.
9. **"Hay powder is now required."** The court: "not prescribed, only permitted," and dosed to each dairy's milk.
10. **"Hand milking is coming back."** The BLW itself said farmers needn't hand-milk again (SRF).
11. **"The holes are bigger in Switzerland because the cheese is better."** Different rulebooks and uses: American Swiss is mostly sliced; the Swiss rule describes wheels.
12. **"Baby Swiss is Swiss."** Invented in Ohio (1960s). Don't bring it in.
