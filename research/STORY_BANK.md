### 1. Why is a dime smaller than a nickel? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** The nickel's big size → a Civil War coin shortage plus one mine owner's lobbying (and 'nickel' means 'copper demon')  
**Category:** History / language · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. Early US coins were worth their metal. The dime was 90% silver, so it could be tiny.
2. Civil War panic: people hoarded silver and even copper coins. Shops made change with stamps and paper 'shinplasters'.
3. Congress wanted coins of a cheap metal nobody would bother hoarding.
4. Joseph Wharton, who owned the Gap nickel mine in Pennsylvania and a refinery, lobbied hard for copper-nickel coins: a 3-cent in 1865, a 5-cent in 1866.
5. A cheap-metal 5-cent coin had to be big to feel worth it, so the 'nickel' outgrew the silver dime.
6. Twist: the nickel is 75% copper. 'Nickel' comes from German 'Kupfernickel' (copper demon), ore that looked like copper but yielded none. Wharton's fortune later founded the Wharton School.

**Sources:** [1](https://coinweek.com/us-coins/coinweek-iq-nickel-joseph-wharton-and-us-coinage/) · [2](https://www.history.com/articles/the-hidden-history-of-the-nickel) · [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel_Mines,_Pennsylvania)

**Demand:** Classic question; several videos and shorts use this exact title (CoinsTV 1.6K, Money Mechanics 1.3K, WhyTunes 1.1K), so people search it.

**Competition:** Gap. Nothing over ~2K views on the exact question. A few low-view AI-style shorts use the hook but none we saw tell the Wharton or copper-demon chain.

**Risks:** Low. Confirm the 'shinplaster' wording and the Kupfernickel etymology (Cronstedt 1751). Sizing by silver content was a convention, not a law.

**Visual (paint path):** Copper-orange splash. Dime and nickel side by side → paint drips into a Civil War coin jar (hoarding) → onto a paper shinplaster → down a Pennsylvania mine shaft to Wharton → into a mint press stamping a fat Shield nickel → back in time to a Saxon miner cursing a goblin over fake copper ore.

### 2. Why does my Wi-Fi cut out when the microwave's on? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** Your Wi-Fi → lives in a 1947 'junk band' set aside for microwave ovens and other noisy machines  
**Category:** Science / design · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. Microwave ovens run at about 2.45 GHz and leak a little of it.
2. Classic Wi-Fi uses the 2.4 GHz band, the same neighborhood.
3. In 1947 an international radio conference in Atlantic City set aside 'ISM' bands for industrial, scientific and medical machines that spray radio noise. 2.45 GHz was one.
4. Because it was so noisy, nobody wanted to license communications there. Engineers called it a junk band.
5. In 1985 the FCC (pushed by engineer Michael Marcus) let anyone use the junk bands without a license, using spread-spectrum radio.
6. In 1997 Wi-Fi (802.11) was built in that free junk band, so your router shares a frequency with your popcorn.

**Sources:** [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISM_radio_band) · [2](https://digitalseams.com/blog/microwave-ovens-wi-fi-and-http) · [3](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20121122-wireless-2-4ghz/)

**Demand:** Very common household question. Dozens of how-to-fix videos and shorts ('Why Does the Microwave Mess With Your Wi-Fi?!' 7.2K).

**Competition:** Gap. Top results are 13K views or less and explain interference, not the 1947/1985 history.

**Risks:** Don't repeat the myth that 2.45 GHz is water's 'resonant frequency'. Stay away from Percy Spencer and the chocolate bar (groove). Deep-verify the 1947 Atlantic City allocation and FCC Gen. Docket 81-413 (1985).

**Visual (paint path):** Electric-blue wave. Router → wave hops into a microwave → zoom out to a 1947 conference table slicing the radio spectrum like a cake, one slice labeled JUNK → a 1985 FCC office opening a gate → laptops and phones pour through.

### 3. Why is there purple glass in old sidewalks? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** Purple sidewalk glass → World War I disrupted the manganese that kept glass clear  
**Category:** Science / history · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. Glass prisms set in 1800s sidewalks ('vault lights') lit basements before electric light.
2. Raw glass is greenish from iron, so makers added manganese, 'glassmaker's soap', to make it clear.
3. Decades of sunlight turn that manganese purple.
4. Much of the manganese came from Germany, and World War I disrupted supply around 1915.
5. US glassmakers switched to selenium, which ages to straw or amber, not purple.
6. So purple glass roughly means pre-1915. Archaeologists and bottle diggers use it to date sites.

**Sources:** [1](https://emberarchaeology.ca/purple-glass-pre-world-war-i/) · [2](https://secure-sha.org/bottle/pdffiles/TheColorPurpleLockhart2006.pdf) · [3](https://anthromuseum.ucdavis.edu/news/november-20-2015-solarized-amethyst-glass)

**Demand:** Strong nearby demand: short 'Why Are Old Bottles Purple?' 1.2M views; 'The Secret Purpose of Sidewalk Glass' 864K. Seattle and SoHo tourists ask about it.

**Competition:** Big shorts answer the nearby questions ('what is it', 'why purple'). The WWI cause seems uncovered. Several near-zero-view AI shorts already use the sidewalk hook.

**Risks:** 'Germany cut off' simplifies things: Lockhart (2006) describes a gradual, economic shift around 1915-1920s. Say 'roughly'. Don't encourage sun-purpling glass to sell as antique.

**Visual (paint path):** The paint IS purple. Sidewalk prism glows → a beam drops into a basement → back up to a glass furnace with a scoop of black manganese → a cargo ship from Germany fades under WWI smoke → the furnace switches to a red selenium jar and new prisms age amber.

### 4. Why do Brits say root beer tastes like medicine? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** Root beer's 'medicine' taste → a 1960 US ban on a sassafras chemical that gave rats liver cancer  
**Category:** Food / science · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. Root beer was flavored with sassafras root bark. Charles Hires sold it as a temperance drink from 1876.
2. Sassafras's main flavor molecule is safrole.
3. Safrole caused liver cancer in lab rats, and the FDA banned it as a food additive in 1960.
4. Makers leaned harder on wintergreen (methyl salicylate) and safrole-free extracts.
5. In Britain, wintergreen is the smell of muscle rubs and medicated products.
6. So Americans taste soda and many Brits taste pharmacy. Same molecule, different childhoods.

**Sources:** [1](https://www.britannica.com/topic/root-beer) · [2](https://library.peoplespharmacy.com/root-beer-flavor-come) · [3](https://culinarylore.com/food-science:the-sassafras-prohibition-is-real-root-beer-actually-banned/)

**Demand:** Huge reaction-video demand: 'British Highschoolers try Root Beer' 3M; 'Irish People Try Root Beer' 1.6M; 'Japan HATES Root Beer' 1.5M; short 'Root beer tastes like toothpaste?' 54K.

**Competition:** Gap. No well-viewed explainer of why. Explainers found were under 10K.

**Risks:** The 'Brits link wintergreen with medicine' point is widely reported but cultural, so find a sensory or flavor-science source. Brand formulas are proprietary, so don't claim what's in A&W. It touches a carcinogen, so keep it factual.

**Visual (paint path):** Brown-gold foam. A glass of root beer → foam trails down to a sassafras root → a lab rat cage stamped BANNED 1960 → a wintergreen sprig → across the Atlantic onto a tube of muscle rub in a British bathroom cabinet → a Brit grimacing at a can.

### 5. Why are there so many acorns this year? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** A bumper acorn crop → more Lyme-carrying ticks about two years later  
**Category:** Nature / ecology · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. Oaks across a region sync up 'mast years': a few lean years, then a glut.
2. The glut swamps seed-eaters so some acorns survive to sprout.
3. White-footed mice feast, survive winter and boom the next year.
4. Larval black-legged ticks feed on the mice and pick up Lyme bacteria.
5. Those ticks become infected nymphs the following year, the stage that bites people most.
6. So Lyme risk tracks acorns with about a two-year lag (Ostfeld, Cary Institute).

**Sources:** [1](https://www.caryinstitute.org/news-insights/podcast/acorns-and-lyme-disease) · [2](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.0040145) · [3](https://medicalresearch.com/more-acorns-more-mice-more-lyme-disease/)

**Demand:** Local TV runs 'why so many acorns?' every mast fall (WCNC, CBS New York, WCCO), and shorts get 0.5K-4.6K. It spikes every few years.

**Competition:** Gap. Exact-question videos are under 5K and none link it to Lyme.

**Risks:** Health topic: no prevention advice. Lyme is mainly a Northeast and Upper Midwest issue. Name deer and climate as other factors so it isn't oversimplified.

**Visual (paint path):** Acorn gold. Acorns raining on a car roof → a mouse stuffing its cheeks → a nest of mice multiplying → a tiny tick on a mouse's ear picks up a speck of paint → calendar pages flip twice → the same speck on a hiker's sock (no gore).

### 6. Why is turning right on red even legal? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** Your right turn on red → the 1973 Arab oil embargo  
**Category:** History / rules · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. For decades most US states banned turning on a red light. Western states, led by California, were the exception.
2. 1973: the OPEC oil embargo brought gas lines and fuel panic.
3. Cars idling at red lights looked like wasted gasoline.
4. The 1975 Energy Policy and Conservation Act pushed states to allow right on red as a condition of federal energy-conservation money.
5. By 1980 all 50 states allowed it.
6. Coda: some cities, like Washington DC, are now banning it again over pedestrian safety.

**Sources:** [1](https://abc7news.com/red-light-right-on-traffic-turn/14343661/) · [2](https://usa.streetsblog.org/2018/05/15/its-time-for-cities-to-rethink-right-turns-on-red) · [3](https://www.slashgear.com/2058696/us-cities-right-turn-on-red-illegal)

**Demand:** A common question from new drivers and Europeans. Driving-test videos on the topic get 100K-1M. Short 'Why NO RIGHT ON RED makes sense' 196K.

**Competition:** Gap on the origin. The exact-hook short we found has 1.2K views.

**Risks:** Live pedestrian-safety debate: state it neutrally and don't moralize. Check California's date and the exact EPCA provision.

**Visual (paint path):** Traffic-light red. Red light → a puff of exhaust → an oil tanker and 1973 gas-line cars → a Congress gavel → a US map with states flipping one by one → a car gliding right.

### 7. Why are there squirrels in every city park? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** City squirrels → released on purpose by 1800s park-makers  
**Category:** Nature / history · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. In early-1800s American cities gray squirrels were rare. The woods were gone and loose squirrels got shot or eaten.
2. In 1847 Philadelphia released squirrels in Franklin Square and fed them. Boston Common and NYC's Central Park followed.
3. Reformers saw tame wildlife as a calming, moral touch of nature for city workers.
4. People fed them, cities planted nut trees and put up nest boxes, and predators were scarce.
5. The population exploded into the squirrels you see today.
6. Victorians also shipped grays to Britain, where they pushed out the native red squirrel.

**Sources:** [1](https://pan-school.sas.upenn.edu/news/etienne-benson-writes-history-american-urban-squirrel) · [2](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/12/131206132408.htm) · [3](https://www.harvardmagazine.com/2013/12/squirrely)

**Demand:** 'Why are there so many squirrels?' is common. NYC squirrel videos get about 47K, and PBS NewsHour covered city squirrels (10K).

**Competition:** Gap. A few small shorts (under 1.5K) touch it and there's no big explainer.

**Risks:** Red-squirrel decline in the UK is also driven by squirrelpox that grays carry, so phrase it accurately. Scholarship rests mainly on one historian (Benson 2013) plus period newspapers.

**Visual (paint path):** Gray-brown fluff. A squirrel on a bench → back to an empty 1840s park → a man opening a crate → people tossing nuts → squirrels spreading across a map → a ship to England where a red squirrel steps aside.

### 8. Why do coins have ridges on the edge? ★ TOP 8

**Link:** Ridged coins and bricked-up British windows → the same 1690s coin-clipping crisis  
**Category:** Design / history · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. Hand-hammered silver coins had rough edges, so people shaved off slivers and melted them.
2. By the 1690s England's coins were badly underweight and trade was choking.
3. In the 1696 Great Recoinage, old coins were recalled and replaced with machine-made coins whose milled edges showed any clipping.
4. The Treasury swallowed the loss from clipped coins.
5. To cover it, Parliament created a window tax (1696).
6. Homeowners bricked up windows to pay less. You can still see the blank windows on old British houses.
7. US dimes and quarters kept the ridges from their silver days.

**Sources:** [1](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/education/resources/georgian-britain-age-modernity/window-tax/) · [2](https://davcoin.com/node/6184) · [3](https://tax.unc.edu/?p=3832)

**Demand:** Huge: short 'Why Quarters Have Ridges (not what you think)' 28M views, plus many more at 1K-11K.

**Competition:** The ridge answer is heavily covered. The window-tax link appears uncovered, and that's our angle.

**Risks:** 'Daylight robbery' coming from the window tax is a myth, so don't use it. Not every blank window is a tax dodge (some are for symmetry). Newton at the Mint is a famous-story groove: a cameo at most.

**Visual (paint path):** Silver. A quarter's ridged edge → shears clipping a hammered coin → slivers into a melting pot → a coin press milling a new edge → a tax collector counting windows → bricks filling a window.

### 9. Why do golf balls have dimples?

**Link:** Golf-ball dimples → a Southeast Asian tree sap that also insulated the first transatlantic telegraph cables  
**Category:** Design / science · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. Before 1848, golf balls were leather pouches stuffed with boiled feathers, and they were pricey.
2. Gutta-percha, a rubbery sap from Palaquium trees in Southeast Asia, reached Britain in the 1840s and was molded into cheap balls ('gutties').
3. Smooth new gutties flew badly. Scuffed, nicked ones flew farther.
4. Makers began hammering and molding patterns on purpose, the ancestors of dimples.
5. The physics: a rough surface keeps air clinging longer, so there's a smaller wake and less drag.
6. The same sap insulated undersea telegraph cables, including the Atlantic cable. Demand stripped the wild trees.

**Sources:** [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golf_ball) · [2](https://historymuseum.ca/cmc/exhibitions/hist/golf/gocan02e.html) · [3](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/americanexperience/features/cable-gutta-percha) · [4](https://engx.theiet.org/b/articles/posts/eccentric-engineer-how-the-golf-ball-got-its-dimples)

**Demand:** Very high: USGA 438K, SciShow 304K, shorts at 499K and 700K.

**Competition:** The physics is heavily covered. The gutta-percha and telegraph history is only lightly covered (around 9K or less).

**Risks:** The story that the first guttie came from packing around a statue shipped from India is legend, so avoid it or flag it. Who first hammered patterns is uncertain.

**Visual (paint path):** White-green sap. A ball on a tee → back to a feather ball being stuffed → a Malaysian tree weeping sap → a smooth ball wobbling in flight → a hammer tapping a pattern → the same sap coating a cable on the ocean floor.

### 10. Where does the 'ding-dong' doorbell tune come from?

**Link:** Your doorbell → a 1793 Cambridge church clock tune, said to come from a phrase in Handel's Messiah  
**Category:** Sound / history · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Chain:**
1. The classic four-note door chime is the 'Westminster Quarters'.
2. It was written in 1793 for the clock of Great St Mary's Church, Cambridge.
3. Tradition says the phrase was developed from Handel's aria 'I know that my Redeemer liveth'.
4. In 1859 it was copied for the Great Clock at Westminster ('Big Ben').
5. Big Ben's fame carried it into grandfather clocks, then electric door chimes and school bells.

**Sources:** [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westminster_Quarters) · [2](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/12442/name-tune-whats-song-clock-chimes-play) · [3](https://www.straightdope.com/21342453/who-wrote-the-clock-chime-tune)

**Demand:** Chime and doorbell videos are popular (shorts at 477K and 985K). 'What tune do clocks play' gets asked on Straight Dope and Mental Floss.

**Competition:** Gap. We found no well-viewed explainer.

**Risks:** Authorship (William Crotch vs Joseph Jowett) and the Handel link are traditional, not proven, so say 'said to'. Light religious content: keep it neutral.

**Visual (paint path):** Brass gold. A finger on a doorbell → sound rings → a grandfather clock → the Big Ben tower → a Cambridge church bell → a page of Handel's score.

### 11. Why are there wild chickens everywhere on Kauai?

**Link:** Kauai's chickens → two hurricanes and a mongoose that never arrived  
**Category:** Nature / ecology · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. Polynesian voyagers brought red junglefowl to Hawaii centuries ago.
2. Farm chickens and gamecocks came later.
3. Hurricanes Iwa (1982) and Iniki (1992) smashed coops. Birds escaped and mixed with the junglefowl, and genetic studies show the hybrids.
4. On other islands, mongooses brought in (1883) to hunt sugarcane rats ate ground-nesting birds' eggs instead. Mongooses hunt by day and rats come out at night.
5. Kauai never got an established mongoose population.
6. No egg thief plus escaped flocks means chickens in every parking lot.

**Sources:** [1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9981564/) · [2](https://www.shakaguide.com/article/kauai/kauai-s-state-bird---that-nobody-wants-around) · [3](https://www.honolulumagazine.com/?p=34783)

**Demand:** A classic tourist question: resort and tour sites have whole pages on it, and shorts reach 26K-41K.

**Competition:** Moderate gap. The top video has 45K (local news) and none centers on the mongoose.

**Risks:** The story that a mongoose bit a dockworker and got thrown overboard is legend, so flag it. Mention cockfighting neutrally or skip it. Confirm Kauai's mongoose status with Hawaii DLNR.

**Visual (paint path):** Rooster red. A rooster on a rental car → an outrigger canoe carrying a junglefowl → a hurricane spiral smashing a coop → a map of the islands with a mongoose stalking every island but Kauai → safe eggs in the grass.

### 12. Why do ladybugs swarm my house every fall?

**Link:** Fall 'ladybug' swarms → Asian beetles brought in to eat aphids, looking for a cliff to winter on  
**Category:** Nature / ecology · **Groove score:** 4/5

**Competition:** Medium. A 324K video covers the exact topic (practical angle). The USDA-and-cliffs chain is less covered.

**Risks:** Disputed: whether wild populations came from the releases or from accidental arrival on freighters (New Orleans). Say so. No pest-control advice.

### 13. Why are old NYC apartments so hot in winter?

**Link:** Your sweltering apartment → radiators sized for open windows during the 1918 flu  
**Category:** History / design · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. Pre-war buildings run steam radiators that are oversized for the rooms.
2. During the 1918 flu, NYC health officials and the 'fresh air' movement had people keep windows open, even in winter.
3. Heating engineers sized systems to heat rooms on the coldest day with the windows open.
4. Fuels changed and windows got tighter, but the big radiators stayed.
5. One-pipe steam is hard to turn down, so tenants open windows in January, just like 1918.

**Sources:** [1](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/apartment-radiator-pandemic-spread/) · [2](https://www.wusf.org/2020-12-10/how-spanish-flu-pandemic-changed-home-heat-radiators) · [3](https://99percentinvisible.org/episode/mini-stories-volume-11/)

**Demand:** A perennial NYC renter complaint and frequent Reddit thread.

**Competition:** Gap on video: exact-question videos are under 1K. NPR and 99% Invisible covered it in audio in 2020.

**Risks:** Rests mainly on heating historian Dan Holohan, so primary engineering manuals are needed. Pandemic topic: no health advice.

**Visual (paint path):** Steam white. A hissing radiator → a tenant opening a window into snow → a 1918 health notice to keep windows open → an engineer's drafting table with a huge radiator → coal scuttle → oil tank.

### 14. Why do old books smell like vanilla?

**Link:** Old-book smell → wood-pulp paper, an idea first floated from watching wasps  
**Category:** Science / language · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. Chemists sniffing old books find vanillin (vanilla's main molecule) and almond-scented benzaldehyde.
2. Vanillin forms as the lignin in wood-pulp paper breaks down.
3. Paper used to be made from rags, and by the 1800s rags ran short.
4. In 1719 the naturalist Réaumur noted that wasps chew wood into paper and suggested people try it. Wood-pulp paper took off in the 1840s.
5. Cheap wood paper brought mass books and newspapers, and the acidic pages that yellow and smell.
6. Twist: for decades much artificial vanilla flavor was made from paper mills' lignin waste.

**Sources:** [1](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/ac9016049) · [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Antoine_Ferchault_de_R%C3%A9aumur) · [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanillin)

**Demand:** High: SciShow 461K, AbeBooks 219K, AumSum 204K, plus many shorts.

**Competition:** The exact question is covered (SciShow 461K), and many AI shorts use the vanilla hook. The wasp and paper-mill-vanilla loop is likely uncovered.

**Risks:** Réaumur's influence on the 1840s inventors was indirect, so don't overclaim. Paper-mill vanillin peaked mid-1900s and most is petrochemical now.

**Visual (paint path):** Vanilla cream. An old book opens and scent wisps rise → the wisps curl into a vanilla pod → a rag-paper mill with empty rag bins → a wasp chewing a fence post into a paper nest → a pulp mill → a newsstand → a paper-mill pipe dripping into a vanilla bottle.

### 15. Why do grackles take over Texas parking lots?

**Link:** Parking-lot grackles → a century-long march north riding irrigation, lawns and lights  
**Category:** Nature / ecology · **Groove score:** 5/5

**Chain:**
1. Around 1900, great-tailed grackles barely reached beyond South Texas.
2. Irrigated farms, cattle feedlots and watered lawns spread food and water north.
3. They spread across the Plains in one of the fastest range expansions of any North American bird.
4. Cities add spilled food and warm pavement.
5. They roost in lit parking-lot trees. One idea (a hypothesis) is that the lights help them spot owls.

**Sources:** [1](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/eead8985-e760-4b16-8c83-3ea475a67e45) · [2](https://allaboutbirds.org/guide/Great-tailed_Grackle) · [3](https://celebrateurbanbirds.org/birds/great-tailed-grackle/) · [4](https://eaglehill.us/URNAonline/articles/URNA-15/04-Luscier.shtml)

**Competition:** Gap. 'Why' videos are under 2K.

### 16. Why are there no billboards in Hawaii?

### 17. Why do Americans call British people 'limeys'?

**Link:** 'Limey' → a cheap switch from lemons to limes that brought scurvy back  
**Category:** Language / history · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. The Royal Navy beat scurvy around 1800 with lemon juice.
2. In the 1860s it switched to West Indian limes from British colonies: cheaper, and still citrus.
3. Limes have much less vitamin C, and the juice was processed in ways (reportedly copper piping) that destroyed more.
4. Sailors still drank lime juice, so 'lime-juicer' and 'limey' stuck.
5. Scurvy returned on Arctic expeditions (Nares, 1875), and doctors concluded citrus didn't work. The confusion lasted until vitamin C was identified in 1932.

**Sources:** [1](https://idlewords.com/2010/03/scott_and_scurvy.htm) · [2](https://www.thenakedscientists.com/node/170327) · [3](https://www.bluesci.co.uk/posts/forgotten-knowledge)

**Demand:** A common question. 'Why Do Americans Call Brits Limeys?' 10K, plus reaction videos.

**Competition:** Gap. Everything we found was 10K or under.

**Risks:** Medical history, not advice. Check the copper-pipe claim and the size of the vitamin C gap. Cegłowski's essay is popular, so it's moderately groove among nerds.

**Visual (paint path):** Lime green. A Brit in a pub with 'limey' in a speech bubble → a sailor drinking his ration → a ledger swapping a lemon for a lime → a copper pipe → a ship frozen in Arctic ice → a 1932 lab flask.

### 18. Why are maraschino cherries that weird bright red?

**Link:** Neon cocktail cherries → Prohibition killed the liqueur and an Oregon lab replaced it  
**Category:** Food / history · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. Original maraschinos were Croatian marasca cherries preserved in maraschino liqueur.
2. Prohibition (1920) banned the liqueur.
3. Oregon State professor Ernest Wiegand developed a brine with calcium salts to firm local cherries without alcohol (about 1925).
4. Brining bleaches the cherries, so makers dye them red and soak them in syrup.
5. Oregon became the hub. The cherry you know is a bleached, dyed Oregon invention.

**Sources:** [1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_H._Wiegand) · [2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maraschino_cherry) · [3](https://blogs.oregonstate.edu/scarc/2013/04/12/) · [4](https://corvallisadvocate.com/the-osu-legacy-of-maraschino-cherries/)

**Demand:** Shorts 'The Truth about Maraschino Cherries' 18K and 'Why You Should Never Buy Maraschino Cherries' 65K.

**Competition:** 'How Maraschino Cherries Are Made' has 85K. The Prohibition-to-OSU chain is less covered.

**Risks:** Food-dye rules (Red No. 3 revoked in 2025) are a live policy topic, so keep it light. No health claims.

**Visual (paint path):** Neon red. A cocktail cherry → a Croatian orchard and liqueur bottle → a 1920 PROHIBITED stamp → a Corvallis lab beaker → a cherry bleached white → dipped red.

### 19. Why does Swiss cheese have fewer holes now?

**Link:** Swiss cheese holes → specks of hay, and spotless modern milking removed them  
**Category:** Science / food · **Groove score:** 3/5

**Chain:**
1. The holes are CO2 bubbles made by Propionibacterium.
2. But gas needs a speck to start a bubble on.
3. In 2015 the Swiss lab Agroscope showed that microscopic hay particles in milk seed the holes.
4. Open buckets in barns used to catch hay dust.
5. Sealed milking machines made milk too clean, so there were fewer holes.
6. Cheesemakers can now add hay dust to dial the holes up or down.

**Sources:** [1](https://www.bostonglobe.com/news/science/2015/05/29/what-causes-holes-swiss-cheese-the-mystery-over/oRKuhOqCJjDgrJZ5DbfIBK/story.html) · [2](https://www.itv.com/news/2015-05-28/mystery-of-swiss-cheese-holes-finally-solved) · [3](https://theworld.org/stories/2016/07/30/century-experts-crack-mystery-holes-swiss-cheese)

**Demand:** High: SciShow 280K, Today I Found Out 276K, AumSum 868K.

**Competition:** The exact question is heavily covered. The 'disappearing holes' angle is partly covered (shorts up to 1.2K).

**Risks:** Low factual risk, high competition.

**Visual (paint path):** Cheese yellow. A slice with holes → a bubble → a hay barn with a bucket catching dust → a steel milking machine → a slice with no holes → a scientist sprinkling hay dust.

### 20. Why is cedar fever so bad in Central Texas?

**Link:** Cedar fever → 1800s overgrazing and stopping wildfires let junipers take over the Hill Country  
**Category:** Nature / ecology · **Groove score:** 5/5

**Chain:**
1. 'Cedar fever' is an allergy to winter pollen from Ashe juniper.
2. Wildfires once swept Hill Country grasslands and killed juniper seedlings.
3. Settlers' heavy grazing removed the grass that fed fires, and fires were put out.
4. Junipers spread into dense 'cedar brakes' across the Hill Country.
5. On windy winter days they release pollen clouds that look like smoke.

**Sources:** [1](https://stateimpact.npr.org/texas/2014/01/08/how-hill-country-grazing-led-to-cedar-fever-in-texas) · [2](https://tpwd.texas.gov/landwater/land/habitats/hillcountry/) · [3](https://www.kvue.com/article/news/local/want-to-see-cedar-trees-in-flames-experts-say-prescribed-burns-could-help-manage-them/269-624040378)

**Demand:** Local TV runs it every winter (FOX 7 Austin, KHOU, FOX 4, CBS Texas). Strong regional interest.

**Competition:** Gap. Local news clips are under 10K, and the 'why' history is rarely told.

**Risks:** Health-adjacent: no medical advice. Regional. Ashe juniper is native and expanded, not invasive. Land-clearing is debated, so stay neutral.

**Visual (paint path):** Pollen orange. A sneeze → a puff of pollen from a juniper → a grassland with a fire line → cattle grazing → juniper sprouts multiplying → a windy day with smoke-like pollen clouds over the hills.
