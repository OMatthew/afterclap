# Short 04 research: "Why are maraschino cherries that weird bright red?"

**Status:** Deep-verified 2026-10-07. This is research only, with no script yet.
**Candidate:** story bank #18.
**Verdict on story bank #18:** keep the topic and change the chain. The story bank's spine, "Prohibition killed the liqueur and an Oregon lab replaced it", is the groove answer, and it doesn't hold up as a cause. Dyed, almond-flavored, alcohol-free "maraschino cherries" were already sold in the US by 1909, and federal chemists were seizing them as fakes eight to ten years before Prohibition. Wiegand's own OSU colleagues said Prohibition had nothing to do with his research. The better and less obvious chain runs through the 1910–1912 court records, the 1912 "imitation" rule, and the 1940 FDA ruling that defined a maraschino cherry as one "dyed red". It ends on a dye that government chemists found in a "maraschino" cherry in 1910: erythrosine, now called FD&C Red No. 3, which is banned from US food from **January 15, 2027**.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

A maraschino cherry has no red of its own. It is stored in a preserving brine of sulfur dioxide (sulfite), which bleaches it pale yellow-white, plus calcium salts that keep it firm. Then it is rinsed, dyed and soaked in sugar syrup with a bitter-almond flavor. The name comes from Zara (now Zadar, Croatia), where dark, sour marasca cherries were kept in maraschino liqueur. The usual story is that Prohibition killed the liqueur and Oregon invented a substitute, but the substitute came first. Between 1909 and 1912, Bureau of Chemistry analysts tested bottles sold as "maraschino cherries" and found Royal Anne cherries in sugar syrup flavored with oil of bitter almond, colored with cochineal or the coal-tar dye **erythrosine**, with no maraschino in them. Makers were fined and their stock was seized. In 1912 the USDA ruled that only marasca cherries in maraschino could use the name; the rest had to say "imitation". Oregon's real contribution came in the late 1920s, when Ernest Wiegand at Oregon State added calcium salts to the brine so soft local cherries stayed firm. Oregon became the industry's center. In 1940 the FDA gave up and adopted what shoppers meant: "maraschino cherries" are cherries "dyed red", sugared and almond-flavored. **The fake took the name, and the bright red is written into the definition.** The 1910 dye, erythrosine, was one of the first food dyes the US approved (1907). As FD&C Red No. 3 it was revoked for food in January 2025, and the ban takes effect January 15, 2027. Most jarred maraschinos already use Red No. 40, so Red 3 lingers mainly in the cherries in canned fruit cocktail, where it was kept because it doesn't bleed into the peaches.

**Recommended chain**
1. The cherry isn't red. A sulfite brine bleaches it pale, calcium keeps it firm, and the red is all dye.
2. The original: marasca cherries from Zara (Zadar) in Dalmatia, kept in maraschino liqueur.
3. Most people say Prohibition killed the liqueur version, so Oregon invented a fake. But the fake came first.
4. ★ By 1909–1911, American "maraschino cherries" were already dyed Royal Annes in almond-flavored syrup with no liqueur in them. Government chemists seized the bottles as fakes.
5. In 1912 the USDA ruled they had to be labeled "imitation".
6. In the late 1920s, Wiegand at Oregon State added calcium to the brine, which firmed soft cherries. Oregon became the hub.
7. In 1940 the FDA flipped: a maraschino cherry is now defined as one "dyed red". The fake won the name.
8. Far end: one 1910 sample was dyed with erythrosine, which is FD&C Red No. 3. It was approved in 1907 and is banned from food from January 15, 2027.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"The fake came first"** or **"long before Prohibition"**, never "Prohibition created the bright-red cherry". It's fine to say "you'll often hear it was Prohibition".
- **"In the late 1920s"** for Wiegand. Sources give 1925, 1925–1931 and 1931.
- **"Calcium keeps them firm"**, not "Wiegand invented the maraschino cherry" or "invented the bleaching". Sulfite brining came before him. His contribution was the calcium.
- **"The brine bleaches them"** is fine, but the sulfite's main job is preserving. Don't say "they bleach them on purpose to make them white" unless you mean the later secondary bleach.
- **"One cherry tested in 1910 was dyed with erythrosine"**, not "maraschino cherries were dyed with Red 3". Other 1911 samples used cochineal.
- **"Most jarred maraschinos switched to Red 40 years ago"**. Red 3 mostly survives in fruit-cocktail cherries. Never say "maraschino cherries are being banned".
- **"Banned from food starting January 2027"**: the order is final and the stay is lifted, but leftover stock made before then can still be sold. Recheck before upload.
- **No health claims.** The FDA says Red 3's rat cancer mechanism doesn't apply to humans; the ban is legal (Delaney Clause), not proof of human harm. Don't say anything about Red 40's safety.
- **"In 1912 the government ruled"**, not "Wiley ruled" or a specific signing day.
- **"Most of America's maraschino cherries"** come from Oregon, not a percentage.

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Prohibition banned the liqueur, so Oregon invented the red cherry."** Dyed, almond-flavored, alcohol-free "maraschinos" were being seized in 1909–1912 ([NJ 912](https://fdanj.nlm.nih.gov/catalog/fdnj00912), [NJ 2392](https://fdanj.nlm.nih.gov/catalog/fdnj02392)), and Wiegand's colleagues said Prohibition had nothing to do with his work ([*The Oregonian* 2006](https://whatscookingamerica.net/History/MaraschinoCherry.htm); [Corvallis Advocate](https://corvallisadvocate.com/the-osu-legacy-of-maraschino-cherries/)). The story bank and [MEL Magazine](https://melmagazine.com/en-us/story/how-are-cocktail-cherries-made-maraschino) repeat it, and it's the groove answer.
2. **"Wiegand invented the maraschino cherry."** He made soft local cherries work by firming them with calcium. Bleaching brines, dye and almond flavor were already in use.
3. **"Maraschino cherries contain Red 3 and are being banned."** Most use Red 40. Red 3 is mostly in fruit-cocktail cherries.
4. **"Red 3 causes cancer in people."** The FDA says the rat mechanism doesn't occur in humans.
5. **"Red No. 4 was allowed in maraschino cherries because the government calls them decorations, not food."** This circulates in a Pavia textbook, MEL Magazine and Snopes-quoted emails. Red 4 was cancelled for food in June 1965 ([TTB circular 65-08](https://www.ttb.gov/images/industry_circulars/archives/1965/65-08.html)); I couldn't verify a maraschino-only exception or the "decorative" reasoning from a primary source. Leave it out.
6. **"They're preserved in formaldehyde / embalming fluid."** A long-running rumor ([MEL Magazine](https://melmagazine.com/en-us/story/how-are-cocktail-cherries-made-maraschino) calls it unfounded). The preservative is sulfite (and historically benzoate).
7. **"Maraschino is the name of the cherry."** The cherry is the marasca. Maraschino is the liqueur.
8. **"Luxardo's cherries were what America drank before Prohibition."** One source says the cherries in 1880s US bars were French imitations (Bordeaux). Unclear, so don't say whose cherries they were.
9. **Exact Wiegand date ("in 1925 he invented...").** See Hedges.
