# Short 15 research: "Why is the Republican party an elephant and the Democratic party a donkey?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-27 for a post around Nov 1-2 (Election Day is Tue Nov 3, 2026, a midterm). Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** "Why is the Republican party an elephant and the Democratic party a donkey?" Neutral by construction if told as a story about cartoons and a newspaper, not about either party. It must treat both evenly; see Neutrality below.
**Verdict:** **Go, but the planned twist is backwards, and the true version is better.** The lead said the elephant cartoon "riffed on" the Herald's Central Park zoo-escape hoax. **It did not. The hoax came after the cartoon.** I read the Harper's Weekly page images: the cartoon is in the issue dated **Nov 7, 1874** (p. 912); the Herald's hoax ran **Mon Nov 9, 1874**. Then **Nast used the hoax against the Herald**, in the issues of **Nov 21** (p. 964: "Caesarism is dead. Now, boys, let the Animals loose in Central Park. That will raise hell!") and **Nov 28** (cover: a scroll of the Herald's "AWFUL CALAMITY" headlines marked "It is a huge hoax"). The zoo-inspired-the-elephant story is repeated by Wikipedia (Republican Party article), Safire (1972), several explainers; the primary sources contradict it. A second surprise, also from the page images: **the donkey in that first elephant cartoon is not the Democrats. It is a newspaper (collar: "N.Y. HERALD") in a fake lion's skin ("CAESARISM"); the elephant is labelled "THE REPUBLICAN VOTE", not the party.** The Democrats are a small animal off to the side. Nast's earlier donkey (Jan 15, 1870) was also a newspaper ("COPPERHEAD PAPERS"). So neither animal began as a party mascot; both began as one cartoonist's jokes about a newspaper and a spooked crowd. **Weak links, do not lean on them:** Jackson putting a donkey on 1828 posters (no poster found, and no 1828 source for the "jackass" label either; the earliest Jackson-as-jackass print I could verify is 1833); official adoption of either animal (no clean source).

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

In the autumn of 1874, the New York Herald (publisher James Gordon Bennett Jr.) had been warning that President Grant might seek a third term and calling it "Caesarism." Thomas Nast, the Harper's Weekly cartoonist, thought the warning was a scare tactic and drew it as the old Aesop fable of an ass in a lion's skin frightening the forest animals. The cartoon, "The Third-Term Panic," is in the Harper's Weekly issue dated Saturday Nov 7, 1874 (p. 912). In it a huge elephant labelled "THE REPUBLICAN VOTE" stampedes toward a pit of broken planks; the ass wears a collar reading "N.Y. HERALD" and a lion's skin reading "CÆSARISM"; a giraffe ("N.Y. TRIBUNE"), a unicorn ("N.Y. TIMES") and an owl ("N.Y. WORLD") are among the frightened animals; a small animal with a "DEMOCRATIC PARTY" badge sits by the pit. The caption: "An Ass, having put on the Lion's skin, roamed about in the Forest, and amused himself by frightening all the foolish Animals he met with in his wanderings.'—SHAKSPEARE or BACON." (A joke: it is Aesop.) Cornell's catalog calls it "widely credited as the first use of an elephant to represent the Republican Party."

That issue was written before the election: its latest news item is dated Oct 22, and its editorials treat the Nov 3 vote as still ahead ("the approaching election in our State"). Then, on Mon Nov 9, the Herald ran a six-column fake front-page story, "AWFUL CALAMITY. The Wild Animals Broken Loose from Central Park" ("Savage Brutes at Large," "Governor Dix Shoots the Bengal Tiger in the Street"). It was a hoax, planned by an editor, Thomas B. Connery, who confessed in Harper's Weekly in 1893: his reason was to scare the zoo's keepers into better safety after he watched a leopard nearly slip loose, and he said nothing about Grant, politics or Nast. Readers panicked; the Times called it an "insane jest." **Nast then took the hoax and stuck it on the Herald**: Nov 21 issue ("The Man Who Laughs," p. 964), Nov 28 cover ("The 'Funny' Little Boy in Trouble"; Minerva: "If you had a little dignity it might prevent your indulging in so many practical jokes."). The myth ("the hoax inspired the elephant") likely comes from Nast's later cartoons plus Safire's 1972 telling that puts Nast's forest "in Central Park."

After the election, Nast's Nov 21 cover and p. 960 cartoon ("Caught in a Trap — The Result of the Third-Term Hoax") put the elephant in the pit. Other cartoonists copied the animals, and over the next years the elephant stopped meaning "the Republican vote" and meant the party; the donkey likewise moved from "a newspaper" to "the Democrats" (Popik finds the first clearly labelled "Democratic donkey" in the NY Daily Graphic in 1876; I did not check it).

**Recommended chain** (six beats, ~65 s; one number per beat at most; beat 6 is the cut if long)

1. ★ **Hook, groove broken in the first line.** "You've heard the elephant came from a fake zoo escape. The dates say the hoax came after." Midterm tie: "It was midterm season, 1874." Number: 1874.
2. ★ **The scare.** A New York newspaper kept warning that the president wanted a third term and called it "Caesarism." A cartoonist thought the warning was a bluff. (Attribute the view: "Nast thought." Do not say the claim was false.)
3. ★ **The cartoon.** Nast drew a donkey in a lion's skin, a fake lion, frightening the animals of a forest. The donkey is the newspaper. A huge elephant, labelled "the Republican vote," panics. Say that the donkey is the paper, not a party. Number: none, or "one cartoon."
4. ★ **The twist.** Two days after that issue's date, the same paper ran a fake story: the zoo animals had escaped and were loose in Central Park. Readers ran. It was a hoax, the paper's own editor later said, to nag the zoo about safety. Nast turned the story on the paper two weeks later. The myth has it backwards. Number: Nov 9.
5. ★ **The donkey too.** Nast's donkey wasn't new: in 1870 he drew a donkey labelled "Copperhead papers" kicking a dead lion ("Hon. E. M. Stanton"). The usual tale gives the donkey to Andrew Jackson, whose opponents called him a jackass; the label is widely repeated, but the oldest print I found of Jackson as a jackass is 1833. (Hedge: "the story says.") Number: 1870 or 1833, one only.
6. **Neutral close.** "Other cartoonists copied the animals until the parties stopped fighting them. Both started as jokes: a spooked giant and a bluffing ass." Optional last line: "Neither started as a party's choice." End on the date or on the animals; no "go vote," no side.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **Order:** "the issue dated Nov 7" and "the hoax on Nov 9." Don't say Nast drew it "the week before the hoax" unless you say "issue date." Harper's dated issues ahead of sale; the Nov 7 issue treats the Nov 3 election as still ahead, so it was written before the hoax either way.
- **Donkey:** "In that cartoon the donkey is a newspaper, not a party." Not "the donkey was always the Democrats."
- **Elephant:** "labelled 'the Republican vote.'" Not "the Republican Party."
- **Why the hoax:** "its editor later said it was a safety warning for the zoo" (Connery, 1893, 19 years on, a self-account). Not "it was meant to hurt Grant" or "it was a joke on voters."
- **Myth credit:** "the story you may have read." Wikipedia, Safire and explainers repeat it. Don't blame a person.
- **Jackson:** "the story goes that Andrew Jackson's opponents called him a jackass, and he liked it." I found a Jackson-as-jackass print from 1833, not 1828. If used, omit the posters.
- **Dates for the vote:** "midterm season, 1874." Most states voted Nov 3; some had voted earlier (the Nov 7 issue covers Ohio and Indiana).
- **Caption:** "Shakspeare or Bacon" is Nast's joke; the fable is Aesop's.

## Neutrality notes
- **Both animals are jokes.** The donkey: an ass in a fake lion's skin (and, in the Jackson story, an insult). The elephant: a giant that stampedes. Give each the same word count and the same tone. Open and close on both.
- **Don't recreate the cartoon's partisan lines.** The pit planks in the Nov 7 cartoon read "INFLATION," "REPUDIATION," "REFORM (TAMMANY K.K.)," "RE-CONSTRUCTION (THE UNION AS IT WAS)," "HOME RULE (THIS IS A WHITE MAN'S GOVERNMENT)," and "CHAOS." These are 1874 partisan slogans, one of them racial. Redraw the animals only; leave the planks out.
- **Don't take Nast's or Harper's side** on the third term; both were pro-Grant. "Nast thought," not "the paper lied."
- **Don't name a winner of the 1874 midterm** or show the Nov 21 pit cartoon as a punch line about one party's loss.
- **Don't put party colors on the animals.** No red elephant, no blue donkey (those colors are post-2000). The brand paint #D9431E goes on the lion's skin, the date, or the newspaper headline, not the elephant. If narration says a color, paint takes it.
- **Check the tie-in**: "midterm season, 1874" is a nod to this week, not to a side.

## Groove risk
- **What generic AI Shorts say:** "Andrew Jackson was called a jackass and put it on his posters; Thomas Nast cartooned both animals in the 1870s; the elephant came from a Herald hoax." Every explainer. They also say "the parties then adopted them."
- **How we differ:** we open with the hoax claim and break it with dates, show the donkey is a newspaper in the very cartoon that "started" the elephant, and end on both animals being jokes. Surprise: the elephant's first cartoon wasn't about the elephant's party and the donkey wasn't the Democrats.
- **Visual groove to avoid:** a red elephant and blue donkey facing off, flags, ballot boxes, a "versus" frame, a zoo gate with silhouettes. Also avoid a Jackson portrait with a donkey; hold it for the "story goes" beat.

## Visual notes (the paint)
- **The forest:** a few hand-inked animals in a loose clearing; a donkey whose lion skin is clearly too big, collar reading HERALD (a plain label, not a logo). Paint (red-lead) lands on the lion's skin when "Caesarism" is spoken, then jumps to the headline AWFUL CALAMITY.
- **Two dates on a paper strip:** "Nov 7" (the issue) and "Nov 9" (the hoax) as two inked ticks; paint hops from one to the other.
- **The newspaper:** a big period headline, "THE WILD ANIMALS BROKEN LOOSE FROM CENTRAL PARK," hand-lettered; a tiny crowd running (faceless, period-neutral).
- **The 1870 donkey:** a small donkey kicking a fallen lion; label "COPPERHEAD PAPERS" on its flank if it has room, or "a newspaper."
- **Avoid:** party colors, planks, the "white man's government" slogan, any faces of Grant or Bennett or Nast, flags.
