# Short 03 research: "Why is some old glass purple?"

**Status:** Deep-verified 2026-10-06. This is research only, with no script yet.
**Promise kept from Short 02:** "And why is some old glass purple? That's another story."
**Verdict on story bank #3:** keep the topic and change the chain. Chain step 4 in the story bank, "much of the manganese came from Germany, and WWI cut it off", is a collector-book myth. It comes from Kendrick (1963), and two peer-reviewed papers in *Historical Archaeology* reject it. The real switch away from manganese came from **bottle-making machines**. That gives us a better and less obvious far end. It also passes the groove check, because "Germany cut off the manganese" is the answer most AI tools and most existing videos give.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

Raw glass is tinted green by iron in the sand. From about the 1870s to around 1920, American glassmakers added a pinch of manganese, called "glassmaker's soap", whose faint pink cancels out the green. Years of sunlight change the manganese, and the glass slowly turns lavender to purple. A Boston glass dealer in 1880 said it was "painted by the magic pencil of the sun". Then the purple stopped. The popular explanation is that World War I cut off German manganese, but Germany supplied only about 2% of US manganese imports. The bigger cause was the automatic bottle machine, which Michael Owens perfected in 1903. Machines melt glass in huge open tank furnaces, where manganese works poorly. Selenium works well there and is needed in tiny amounts, so through the 1910s, as machines took over, glassmakers switched to selenium. Selenium ages to a faint straw color, not purple. Owens had started work in a glass factory at **age 10**, carrying red-hot bottles, and in 1913 the National Child Labor Committee said his machine had done more to end child labor in glass factories than their campaigns for new laws. **So purple glass roughly marks the hand-made era, and the machine that ended it also took children out of bottle factories.**

**Recommended chain**
1. Old clear glass (bottles, sidewalk prisms) is often purple. It was made clear and the sun changed it.
2. Iron in sand makes glass green. Manganese, "glassmaker's soap", cancels the green.
3. UV light slowly turns that manganese purple. Glass from a half-buried bottle turns purple only above the soil line.
4. Most people say WWI cut off German manganese. That's a myth: Germany was a minor supplier.
5. The real switch came from bottle machines (Owens, 1903). Manganese failed in their tank furnaces, so makers used selenium, which goes straw, not purple.
6. So purple roughly means made before about 1920, in the hand-blown era.
7. Far end: Owens started work at 10 as a "carry-in boy", and his machine helped end child labor in bottle factories.

**Other far ends we considered (all sourced, all weaker)**
- *The myth's own origin:* the "Germany" story comes from a 1963 collector's book (Kendrick). It works well as one beat, but not as the ending.
- *Selenium came from copper waste:* the US Tariff Commission (1918) called selenium "a domestic by-product of copper". It's neat but abstract.
- *Beacon Hill status symbol:* owners first hated the purple panes and later prized them. In 1917 an architect put old purple panes into a new house and people wrote to the newspaper asking if they were fake. This is a separate story about the early 1800s (see the table) and could be its own Short later.
- *Faraday studied purple London windows in 1823:* famous-scientist groove, so skip it.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"Around World War One"** and **"by about 1920"**, never "in 1915" or "after 1915". The switch ran from about 1910 to 1930, and drugstore bottles kept manganese until about 1924.
- **"Roughly"** or **"usually"** before any dating rule. Purple is one clue, not a date stamp.
- **"Helped end child labor in bottle factories"**, not "ended child labor". Laws and other machines also played a part, and the NCLC quote comes to us through secondary sources.
- **"Machines took over"**, not "the machine arrived and manganese vanished". It was gradual, and some early machine-made bottles still contain manganese.
- **"Germany was never the main supplier"** if we bust the myth. Don't say Germany supplied none: Germany supplied about 2%, and glassmakers said they bought "some" from Germany.
- **"Cancels" or "hides" the green**, not "removes" it.
- **"A faint straw color"** for selenium, not "amber".
- **Sidewalk glass:** don't date it as precisely as bottles. A safe line is "most of it went in before electric light won out". Some purple prisms are modern replacements tinted to match.
- Don't say how deep the purple is tells you its age. It depends on sun and manganese content.
- Don't encourage people to sun-purple or irradiate glass to sell it as antique (collectors do this: [UC Davis](https://anthromuseum.ucdavis.edu/news/november-20-2015-solarized-amethyst-glass); [SHA](https://sha.org/bottle/colors.htm)).

## Myths to avoid
1. **"WWI cut off German manganese, so purple means pre-1915."** This is Kendrick (1963), and it's repeated by Wikipedia, UC Davis and Ember Archaeology. Germany supplied 2% of US manganese imports in 1910, and the main cause of the switch was machines ([Lockhart](https://secure-sha.org/bottle/pdffiles/TheColorPurpleLockhart2006.pdf); [Miller & Pacey](https://secure-sha.org/bottle/pdffiles/MillerPacey1985.pdf)).
2. **"The purple was intentional."** Owners believed this in 1880. Some glass is deliberately amethyst (manganese used as a colorant), but sun-purple glass began clear.
3. **"Darker purple = older."** Depth depends on sun exposure and manganese content.
4. **"All old clear glass turns purple."** Only glass with manganese does. Lead crystal and selenium glass don't, and buried manganese glass stays clear. So clear old glass does not mean post-1920.
5. **"Beacon Hill's purple came from one bad 1818–1824 shipment from [Hamburg / England / France]."** The country changes with each teller.
6. **"Every purple sidewalk prism is antique."** Restorations use new pink-tinted glass, and Astoria's purple prisms date from 1924.
7. **The Keating–Owen Child Labor Act (1916) was named after Michael Owens.** It wasn't: it was named for Senator Robert L. Owen. Keep the two apart.
8. **"Owens invented the bottle machine."** He built the first commercially successful *fully automatic* one. Semi-automatic machines came first.
9. **"Selenium turns glass amber."** It gives only a faint straw tint, mostly in the thick parts.

## Candidate hooks (questions viewers actually ask)
1. "Why are old glass bottles purple, but new ones never are?" (11 words. It matches the 1.2M-view "Why Are Old Bottles Purple?" demand and sets up the switch.)
2. "Why is the glass in old sidewalks purple?" (8 words. The Seattle/SoHo tourist question and the story bank's hook. Don't use it to date the sidewalk exactly.)
3. "Why do bottles left in the desert turn purple?" (9 words. A real bottle-digger and desert-hiker question. It shows that sunlight is the cause right away.)
