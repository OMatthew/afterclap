# Short 15 research: "Why are Republican states red and Democratic states blue?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-28 for a post around Nov 1-2, 2026 (Election Day is Tue Nov 3, a midterm). Research only, no script yet. Note: `15-party-animals.md` already uses slot 15 (elephant and donkey). Rename one file or one slot when the plan is set.
**Candidate:** "Why are Republican states red and Democratic states blue?" Neutral by construction if told as a story about TV graphics departments, newspaper map desks and a long count. Neither party is the subject, and the colors were never chosen by either one. See Neutrality below.
**Verdict:** **Go, but the lead needs three fixes, and the true chain is better than "the recount did it."** (1) **"The 2000 networks happened to all use red for Republicans" is half wrong.** They did not stumble into it that year. Over sixteen years the networks drifted there one at a time and by copying each other: ABC red for Republicans in 1980, CBS in 1984, CNN in 1992, NBC last (1994 midterms or 1996; one source says 2000). NBC's executive said it was "so as not to have a confusion for the viewers." There was no coordination (CNN). But **print had not moved**: *Time* drew Democrats red in every election from 1988 to 2000, and the *Washington Post*'s 2000 election graphics did the same. (2) **"The big blue Reagan map described as suburbia" is a misquote.** NBC's David Brinkley called the 1980 map "a suburban swimming pool." (3) **The "colors alternate by incumbent party" rule that many pages repeat is a myth** (a blog commenter's theory, never confirmed; Smithsonian says it "doesn't appear to be the case for every election," and a network-by-network video review contradicts it). The strongest true links are all checkable: **NBC's 1976 map and why it was blue for Republicans (Britain)**; **the first print use of "red states" and "blue states" I can find, Boston Globe, Oct 15, 1992, meant the opposite**; **Tim Russert on the *Today* show, Oct 30, 2000: "those remaining 61 electoral red states, if you will?"**; **a count that lasted five weeks (Nov 7 to Dec 12) with the same map on screen**; **the *New York Times* graphics editor's reason: "'red' begins with 'r,' 'Republican' begins with 'r.' ... There wasn't much discussion about it."** The payoff is a mechanism, not "it's backwards": *nobody decided*. The color of each party was a flip of a network coin, then copied, then frozen by five weeks of the same map. Four years after "if you will," the American Dialect Society made "red/blue/purple state" its Word of the Year.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

Neither party has ever had an official color (CNN, 2024). Before 2000, which color a state wore depended on the channel and the year. In **1976** NBC built a 14-foot-high, 24-foot-long lit-up map of plastic states for Studio 8H. The head of its election unit, Roy Wetzel, explained the colors this way: "Great Britain. Without giving it a second thought, we said blue for conservatives, because that's what the parliamentary system in London is, red for the more liberal party. And that settled it. We just did it." That night NBC called the election for Jimmy Carter at 3:30 a.m. Eastern, when Mississippi lit up red. So NBC's Republican color was **blue** and its Democratic color **red**, and that's what NBC used through 1992 (Smithsonian: NBC did not switch in the four elections Wetzel ran, 1976 to 1988).

The other networks chose for themselves. In 1976 ABC used yellow for Ford and blue for Carter. By 1980 ABC flipped to red for Republicans. ABC's Frank Reynolds said on air that "the map is predominantly red, because red is the color for Reagan on our map" (from a video review; not seen). CBS followed by 1984; CNN in 1992 (it had used blue for Republicans in 1988). NBC held out alone until the mid-1990s. So on election night 1992 a viewer who flipped channels saw the same state change color. NBC's William Wheatley later said the network decided to sync with the others "so as not to have a confusion for the viewers." CNN: "There is no evidence that the major networks actively coordinated; they may simply have copied one another."

Print was not in on it. *Time* gave Democrats red and Republicans blue in its election graphics "in every election from 1988 to 2000," and the *Washington Post*'s 2000 election graphics did too (Farhi, 2004). The phrase "red state" in today's sense barely existed. The earliest print use that Kevin Drum found in a Nexis search (2004) is a Boston Globe column of **Oct 15, 1992**: "the red states for Clinton start swamping the blue states for Bush." **Red meant the Democrat.** (William Safire in the *New York Times*, Oct 3, 2004, quoted the same line and relayed a reader's finding that its writer was "the coiner, or at least an early user.") Drum found only one other U.S. print use for the rest of the 1990s.

Then **Oct 30, 2000**, a week before the vote, on NBC's *Today* show, Tim Russert and Matt Lauer talked over a color-coded projection map that had first appeared days earlier on MSNBC. Russert: "So how does he get those remaining 61 electoral red states, if you will?" The "if you will" is the tell that it was new. Washington Post researchers could find nothing earlier in a database of newspapers, magazines and TV transcripts since 1980. Russert himself said, "I'm sure I wasn't the first to come up with it."

**Election night, Tue Nov 7, 2000**, did not end the contest. Florida's certified margin was 537 votes (Nov 26); the Florida courts and the U.S. Supreme Court took it to **Tue Dec 12**, five weeks to the day. The next day the Vice President conceded. For those five weeks the electoral map was on TV and in print every day. Drum: "Usually we see it for a day or two and then it's gone." Two days after the vote the *New York Times* and *USA Today* ran county-by-county color maps, both red for Bush and blue for Gore. Archie Tse, then a senior graphics editor at the *Times*: "I just decided 'red' begins with 'r,' 'Republican' begins with 'r.' It was a more natural association. ... There wasn't much discussion about it." *USA Today*'s Paul Overberg: "The reason I did it was because everybody was already doing it that way at that point." Hosts and comics picked up the words (Letterman, a week after the vote, on making Bush "president of the red states and Al Gore head of the blue ones"). By 2004 the phrase was the American Dialect Society's Word of the Year.

So the true answer to "why": **no one decided.** Networks copied each other toward red-Republican between 1980 and 1996. Print copied the networks in a hurry in 2000. A five-week count froze it. The Oxford English Dictionary's own note: "The colour designation is an arbitrary one, and indeed the attribution of the two colours had been reversed in media coverage of various elections prior to 2000." And the rest of the world does it the other way: red is the left almost everywhere (Britain's Labour is red, the Conservatives blue), which is the very reason NBC chose blue for Republicans in 1976.

## Recommended chain (six beats, about 65 s; one number per beat at most)

1. ★ **Hook, groove broken at once.** "Until the mid-1990s, whether a state was red or blue depended on which channel you were watching." (No number on screen, or "1996" as a year card.) Hedge: "the mid-1990s," not an exact year, because NBC's switch is dated 1994, 1996 or 2000 by different sources.
2. ★ **NBC, 1976.** A TV studio builds a lit-up map of plastic states. Blue for Republicans, red for Democrats, "because that's what the parliamentary system in London is." The picture: the map, a Mississippi that lights up red at 3:30 in the morning. Number: **1976.**
3. ★ **Everyone else picks their own.** ABC starts with yellow for the Republican, then goes red in 1980. CBS and CNN follow later. NBC holds out. Flip channels in 1992 and the same state changes color. Number: **1980** (ABC's switch). Optional line: an ABC anchor explained red as the color "for Reagan on our map" (verify the clip before use).
4. ★ **The phrase is brand new, and print hasn't moved.** *Time* magazine still draws Democrats red; so do the *Washington Post*'s graphics. Then, a week before the vote, a morning show: Tim Russert asks how a candidate gets "those remaining 61 electoral red states, if you will?" Number: **61.** (Optional extra, only if the pairing doesn't crowd the beat: the earliest print use anyone found, 1992, had red for the Democrat.)
5. ★ **Five weeks.** Election night, and no winner. The same map on screen every night. Two days in, the *Times* and *USA Today* run color county maps. The *Times* graphics editor: "red begins with r, Republican begins with r." "There wasn't much discussion about it." Number: **five weeks.**
6. **Payoff, concrete and quiet.** "Nobody voted. No party picked. Networks copied, a recount froze it, and four years after a host said 'if you will,' 'red state, blue state' was the American Dialect Society's Word of the Year." Number: **four years.** Optional sting in place of the last clause, only if Leip's FAQ is checked: "One of the oldest election databases never switched. It still paints Republicans blue." End on the image of a map. No moral, no "get out and vote."

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"Until the mid-1990s."** NBC's switch is dated 1994 (Eldorado, midterms), 1996 (LA Times, CNN) or 2000 (Smithsonian). Don't name the exact year.
- **"The earliest I can find" / "an early use"** for the 1992 Globe column and for Russert's *Today* phrase. "First in a database search" is the claim; Russert himself said he wasn't first.
- **"Five weeks."** Don't say "36 days" unless attributing to Farhi.
- **Tse is a recollection**, quoted in Smithsonian. Say "the *Times* graphics editor said" and keep it to his own words.
- **"The networks copied each other"** is right; "agreed," "decided together" and "coincidence" are not (CNN: no evidence of coordination; Drum's 2004 "happened to" is a guess).
- **Don't state why ABC chose red.** The "R for Reagan" lines are anchors' on-air remarks, one unseen and one from a CNN article.
- **The 1976 map melting** is "executives claim." Skip it unless the beat has room.
- **Don't call anyone's choice wrong or revealing.** The point is that nobody chose.

## Neutrality (this Short's one hard rule)
- Treat both parties evenly: **both colors and both parties appear in the same sentence wherever possible.** No party is the subject. The subjects are networks, newspapers and a count.
- **Describe the 2000 dispute strictly as dates and a procedure:** election day Nov 7; Florida certification Nov 26 by 537 votes; litigation; the U.S. Supreme Court's decision Dec 12 ended the recount; the next day the losing candidate conceded. **No** "stolen," "handed," "decided by," "popular vote winner," "5-4," "ideological" or who-was-right.
- **No 2026 predictions,** and no mention of this week's election or its results. Don't show any real recent-election map or candidate faces. Use an abstract or unlabeled map and the 1976 studio as the picture.
- **Keep the Cold War ("Reds") angle out.** It reads as a comment on one party. Don't repeat Sabato's theory or Todd's "always made the other guys red."
- Don't use the California Republican Party's 2014 switch to blue, Obama's 2004 convention speech, or "red state / blue state" culture clichés (churches and NASCAR, latte-drinking). Those are about people, not colors.
- Use the surnames "Bush" and "Gore," "Carter" and "Ford," "Clinton" and "Bush" only as the names on the map; don't characterize them.

## Myths and traps to avoid
1. **"The colors have always been red for Republicans."** No: red-Democrat/blue-Republican was common on U.S. TV through the early 1990s, and the 1883 Census atlas had it that way too.
2. **"Tim Russert coined red and blue states."** He popularized it (Safire) and said he wasn't first. A Boston columnist used the words in 1992.
3. **"The alternating-incumbent rule."** A blog commenter's theory (2004). Drum himself never got confirmation; Smithsonian says it doesn't fit every election.
4. **"The networks all used red for Republicans in 2000, by chance."** They converged over 1980 to 1996 by copying. Print and *Time* and the *Post* did not.
5. **"NBC's 1976 map was the first."** CBS used regional color maps in 1972 (Eldorado).
6. **"The NYT picked red for Republicans in 2000, so everyone followed."** Tse says there was no discussion; *USA Today*'s Overberg says he followed what was already done. Holland credits *USA Today*'s map. Order is murky.
7. **"Brinkley called the Reagan map suburbia."** It was "a suburban swimming pool" (NBC, 1980).
8. **"The Short's answer is the Cold War."** There's no evidence networks chose colors that way (CNN).
9. **"Texas ballots were the origin."** A local boss-rule system (Handbook of Texas); not maps.
10. **"The recount alone explains it."** The recount froze a scheme that was already mostly in place on TV, and print matched it in a hurry.

## Groove risk
- **What a generic AI Short says:** "Red is for Republicans and blue for Democrats because of the 2000 election, when Tim Russert coined the terms and the networks picked red and blue; in Europe red is for the left, so America is the opposite." Smooth, partly wrong, and the "it's backwards" payoff the channel owner dislikes.
- **How we differ:** open with a testable surprise (it depended on the channel), give one clean reason (Britain, 1976), show the quiet copying, then land the 2000 count as a *mechanism* with two voices (Russert's "if you will" and the *Times* editor's "not much discussion"). The payoff is that no one decided, shown by a Word of the Year four years after an apology.
- **Visual groove to avoid:** red and blue paint splashed over a US outline, Capitol dome, stars and stripes, donkey and elephant (that's the party-animals Short), Uncle Sam, ballot boxes. Keep it dry, like a TV station basement.

## Visual notes (the paint)
- **The brand paint is red-lead #D9431E, and the channel rule says paint takes the color the narration names.** Here the paint should go red or blue exactly as the narration says it (blue for Republicans in the 1976 beat, red for them later). Keep the brand dot red.
- **Paper strip:** (beat 1) three small TV sets side by side, each showing the same unlabeled map in different colors; (beat 2) a studio wall of plastic states with bulbs behind them; (beat 3) a row of TV sets with one state changing from blue to red as you move across; (beat 4) a magazine map and a morning-show couch with a hand-drawn "61"; (beat 5) a calendar of five weeks of the same map (five little maps in a row); (beat 6) a dictionary page or a word on a wall, with a tiny "if you will" scrawl.
- **Map:** use an abstract, unlabeled shape or stylized rectangles, **not a real recent-election map** (neutrality). Don't fill all states in one party's color at the end.
- **Title cards (years only):** 1976, 1980, 61, 5 WEEKS. No logos (NBC, ABC, CBS, CNN, *Times*, *USA Today*); describe them by name in narration.
- **Cover words (2-4):** **"RED OR BLUE?"**, **"IF YOU WILL"**, **"5 WEEKS"**, **"IT DEPENDED ON THE CHANNEL."** Avoid "REPUBLICAN" and "DEMOCRAT" on the cover.
