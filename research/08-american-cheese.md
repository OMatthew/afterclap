# Short 08 research: "Why does American cheese melt so smoothly?"

**Status:** Deep-verified 2026-10-10, before Short 07's voice. Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** the link out of 07 named in 07's draft last line ("And why does American cheese melt so smoothly? That's another story."). Not in the story bank; not on the groove list, but the hook is groove-adjacent (see Myths).
**Verdict:** safe to tease as written. The premise is true and primary: melting salts (citrates and phosphates) pull calcium off the cheese proteins, the loosened proteins hold the fat, and the melt stays smooth; the US standard of identity lists the 13 permitted salts and calls the result "a homogeneous plastic mass." The bridge from 07 is real and better than hoped: **processed cheese was invented in Thun, Switzerland, by an Emmentaler export firm, as grated Emmentaler melted with a citrate salt, so that Emmentaler could cross the ocean to America without spoiling.** Two corrections to the brief: (1) **Kraft's 1916 patent has no melting salt.** It is heat-and-stir sterilization of cheddar in cans or jars (175 °F for 10–15 minutes); his 1920 and 1921 patents don't have one either. (2) The first US patent I found for a melting salt in cheese (Feb. 15, 1921) belongs to Kraft's rival, the Phenix Cheese Company, and it sells the salt as "a mild laxative and a liver tonic." Kraft merged with Phenix in 1928. **Recommended far end:** that patent. Second far end (optional last line): Kraft Singles haven't been allowed to call themselves "cheese food" since 2002, because an FDA inspection found an ingredient the rulebook doesn't list.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

Cheese is fat held in a net of protein, and calcium is the glue in the net. Heat an aged cheese and the net tightens and squeezes: the fat runs out as grease, the protein clumps into strings. American cheese doesn't do that because it was melted once already, in a factory, with a pinch of a melting salt (sodium citrate or a sodium phosphate). The salt grabs the calcium, the protein net loosens and soaks up water, and the loosened protein wraps the fat in small droplets. Melt it again and nothing separates. Federal law says exactly which salts are allowed (13 of them, up to 3 percent by weight) and defines the result as "a homogeneous plastic mass." The idea is Swiss. Gerber & Co. of Thun had exported Emmentaler wheels since 1836, and whole wheels kept spoiling on the long sea crossing, so from 1905 Walter Gerber and his manager Fritz Stettler worked on "a durable cheese preserve for export to the tropics." By 1911 they had it: grated Emmentaler melted with a citrate salt, poured as a small wheel into a tin. Switzerland's official culinary-heritage inventory says this "made it possible to export cheese to America without it spoiling on the long crossing." Then the war shut Swiss exports down completely. In Chicago, James L. Kraft got his patent on June 6, 1916, and it isn't the one people think: no salt at all, just cheddar melted, stirred and sealed in cans, so it "may be kept indefinitely without spoiling." The US Army bought about six million pounds of it in tins. The salt came into American patents from Kraft's rival, Phenix Cheese of New York, on Feb. 15, 1921: George Garstin's patent says heat makes "the butter fats separate from the casein and float upon the surface," that "medicinal sodium phosphate" keeps the cheese "smooth and homogeneous," that the same salt "has the properties of a mild laxative and a liver tonic," and that "the precise manner in which the sodium phosphate acts upon the cheese is problematical." Kraft merged with Phenix in 1928. Today the salt is in every slice, and the government has long since fixed the names: "pasteurized process American cheese" for the real thing (made of cheddar, Colby, washed-curd or granular cheese), "cheese food" when at least half is cheese and the rest is milk, cream or whey. In December 2002 an FDA inspection found milk protein concentrate in Kraft Singles and Velveeta, an ingredient the "cheese food" and "cheese spread" rules don't list, so the FDA wrote that they were misbranded. Kraft kept the ingredient and changed the name instead, to "pasteurized prepared cheese product," a name no rule defines. That's why a Kraft Single says "cheese product." Not because it isn't cheese, but because it has something extra in it.

**Recommended chain** (eight beats; cut 3 or 7 if the read runs long)
1. The mechanism: cheese is fat in a protein net glued by calcium. Heat it and the net squeezes: grease out, protein in strings. American cheese was melted once already, with a melting salt that takes the calcium; the loosened protein holds the fat, so it never splits.
2. ★ The idea is Swiss. An Emmentaler export firm in Thun kept losing wheels on the ocean crossing. By 1911 Gerber and Stettler had melted grated Emmentaler with a citrate salt and sealed a small wheel in a tin: cheese that could reach America.
3. The war shut Swiss exports down completely. (Optional, one line; it hands off to Kraft.)
4. ★ Chicago, 1916: James Kraft's patent. No salt in it. Melt cheddar, stir, seal in cans so it keeps "indefinitely." The Army bought about six million pounds in tins.
5. ★ The salt reached American patents in 1921, from Kraft's rival, Phenix: sodium phosphate keeps the fat from floating off the cheese, and, the patent adds, it's "a mild laxative and a liver tonic." It admits nobody knew why it worked. Kraft merged with Phenix in 1928.
6. Today the law lists 13 melting salts and calls the result "a homogeneous plastic mass." Name beat: "pasteurized process American cheese."
7. Far end B: in 2002 the FDA found an unlisted ingredient (concentrated milk protein) in Kraft Singles and said they couldn't be called "cheese food." Kraft kept the ingredient and changed the name to "cheese product." That's the only reason the wrapper says it.
8. Last line options: (a) "So the smoothest cheese in America was invented to get Swiss cheese to America." (b) The laxative line, if beat 5 is moved to the end.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"Melting salt"** (the law says "emulsifying agent"; scientists say "calcium sequestering salt"). Say "a salt" and name one ("sodium citrate" or "sodium phosphate"); never "chemicals."
- **The salt doesn't emulsify; the loosened protein does.** "The salt grabs the calcium, and the loosened protein holds the fat."
- **"By 1911"** for the Swiss invention (sources give 1906, 1911, 1913). **"Gerber and Stettler"** or "a cheese firm in Thun." Never "Gerber invented" alone (HLS credits Stettler).
- **"Melted grated Emmentaler with a citrate salt"**: Emmentaler is in the Swiss heritage entry (flavour), Emmi (shelf life of Emmentaler) and NDB (an Emmentaler export firm). Citrate is in Ustunol, Dairy Consultant and the Thun page, not in NDB.
- **"So Emmentaler could cross the ocean"**: the federal heritage inventory says "to America"; NDB says "the tropics"; de.wikipedia says "overseas." "To America" is fair with the inventory as the source; "across the ocean" is the safest.
- **"Kraft's 1916 patent"**: heat, stir, seal, no salt. Don't say he "invented American cheese" or "added sodium phosphate" (his patents don't).
- **"One of the first American patents for the salt"** (Garstin/Phenix, 1921). Not "the first" (unverified).
- **"A mild laxative and a liver tonic"**: quote the patent; don't say the cheese *was* a laxative.
- **"Kraft merged with Phenix in 1928"** (Encyclopedia of Chicago: "a merger with Phenix Cheese created Kraft-Phenix"). Not "bought".
- **"About six million pounds"** for the Army, "in tins"; sources vary (5 to 6 million).
- **"The law"** is fine here (21 CFR 133 is a binding standard of identity), unlike 07's USDA grade. **"Pasteurized process American cheese"** is the exact legal name.
- **Kraft Singles:** "an ingredient the rule doesn't list" (milk protein concentrate). Never "not real cheese," never a percentage of cheese in Singles (no source gives one).
- **"Cheese product" has no legal definition** (Farm and Dairy; culture). Don't call it "illegal" or "banned."
- **"Homogeneous plastic mass"**: if used, add "plastic as in mouldable."
- **No "chain"** in the narration (the paint carries it).

## Myths to avoid
1. **"American cheese is plastic / not real cheese."** Pasteurized process American cheese is cheese (cheddar, Colby, washed curd or granular) plus up to 3% melting salt and small optional extras, by law. The "plastic" in the law means mouldable.
2. **"Kraft invented processed cheese."** Thun, by 1911, from Emmentaler. Kraft's 1916 patent is the first US one, and it's a sterilizing process.
3. **"Kraft's trick was sodium phosphate / sodium citrate."** Not in his patents. The salt patent in the US was Phenix's (1921); Kraft merged with Phenix in 1928.
4. **"Sodium citrate = NaCHO."** True and worn out. Skip.
5. **"Kraft Singles are less than 51% cheese, so they can't be called cheese."** The 2002 letter was about milk protein concentrate, an unlisted ingredient, not the cheese percentage. No source gives the percentage.
6. **"The FDA banned Kraft from calling Singles cheese."** It said they were misbranded as "cheese food"; Kraft chose a new, undefined name.
7. **"American cheese is called American because Kraft made it."** The name was English slang for Yankee cheddar by around 1800.
8. **"The Swiss invented it for fondue."** Wikipedia says fondue "influenced" them; the sources that matter (NDB, the heritage inventory, Emmi) say export and shelf life.
9. **"Swiss cheese in a can inspired Kraft."** One hedged mention (Invention & Technology). Don't use.
10. **"Kraft invented the wrapped slice."** Clearfield Cheese (Pennsylvania) sold individually wrapped slices in 1956; Kraft Singles came in 1965. Kraft's 1950 De Luxe were packaged slices, not individually wrapped.
11. **"Velveeta was Kraft's."** Emil Frey at the Monroe Cheese Company, Monroe, N.Y., 1918; a separate Velveeta company from Feb. 1923; Kraft bought it in 1927 ([Wikipedia, Velveeta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velveeta), citing NYT 1923 and the Middletown Times Herald 1927; not opened). Keep Velveeta out.
12. **"The emulsifying salt is an emulsifier."** It frees the casein, which does the emulsifying.

## Bridge from Short 07

**Short 07's last scene (narration v1):** "So today, the smallest hole in real Emmentaler is about the biggest one in America's top-grade Swiss." Then: "And why does American cheese melt so smoothly? That's another story."

**Is there a real shared thing?** Yes, three, and one is a material on both sides.
- **Real, the material: Emmentaler.** 07 is about Emmentaler's holes. 08 opens its history with grated Emmentaler melted with a citrate salt in Thun, by an Emmentaler export firm (HLS, NDB, the heritage inventory, Emmi). The first processed cheese *was* Emmentaler.
- **Real, the object: the slice and the slicer.** 07 ends on holes shrunk for slicing machines. 08's Kraft loaf (1921) was sized to be "cut into slices of the right size to make ordinary sandwiches," and the product everyone pictures is a slice.
- **Real, the idea: a Swiss cheese remade to survive a machine or a journey.** 07: America shrank the holes so the cheese survives the slicer. 08: Thun melted the cheese so it survives the ocean, and then America made it its own. In both, a rulebook then fixed what the cheese is (USDA grade in 07; FDA standard of identity in 08).
- **Real, the irony (one line, if wanted):** the Swiss invented melted-and-tinned cheese so Emmentaler could reach America; America ended up with "American cheese" made of cheddar.
- **Not real (don't reach for it):** no person or company links the two (Gerber never touched the holes; Kraft never touched Emmentaler). Thun and Bern are both in 07's orbit (the agriculture office is in Bern) but that's geography, not a bridge.

**Is the tease true as asked?** Yes. American cheese melts smoothly by design, and the mechanism is settled science and codified in law. "American cheese" is the right everyday name and the exact legal one ("pasteurized process American cheese"). One listening risk: the tease follows "America's top-grade Swiss," so a listener who hears "American cheese" as "American Swiss" could blur them. **Clearer, equally true:** "And why does a slice of American cheese melt so smoothly? That's another story." Both are safe; the "a slice of" version also puts 08's object (the slice) in the line. Other honest variants: "And why doesn't American cheese go greasy when it melts?" / "And why does American cheese melt but cheddar splits?"

**Write backward (plant it in 07):** in 07's slicing-machine beat, let one clean slice drop onto a sandwich. In 07's last scene (two wedges, Swiss and American), set a square slice, or a small round tin, beside the American wedge; when the tease plays, the red drop slides from the wedge onto the slice and spreads smooth. No colour is called out, so the paint stays red.

## Backup (thread rule "two ahead")

**Candidate: "Why does Gruyère have no holes?"** (bridge from 07: a second Swiss wheel; 07 already uses "blind" as the USDA's word for a holeless Swiss; and the same Swiss-rulebook-vs-American-rulebook shape.)

**Verdict (one paragraph):** usable, and almost untold. Premise verified: real Gruyère has few or no holes. Agroscope's 2015 release says Gruyère and Sbrinz have no holes because their lactic bacteria make very little CO2 ([Agroscope, May 28, 2015, supplement](https://www.newsd.admin.ch/newsd/message/attachments/39613.pdf)), and the Gruyère AOP rulebook (cahier des charges) says eyes are "desirable but not indispensable," holes are mostly 4–6 mm, holes over 6 mm are a defect, and the top grading score for eyes is "ouverture rare ou aveugle," rare eyes or blind ([Gruyère AOP, cahier des charges, Art. 5 §2, Art. 41](https://gruyere.com/fr/cahier-des-charges)). Two independent sources for the central link, both primary. The far end is American: the US federal standard of identity for "gruyere cheese" says "It contains small holes or eyes," is made with "propionic acid-producing bacterial cultures" (the gas-makers of 07) and names no country ([21 CFR 133.149](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/133.149)); and on March 3, 2023 the Fourth Circuit held that "the term 'GRUYERE' is generic as a matter of law" because "cheese consumers in the United States understand 'GRUYERE' to refer to a type of cheese," after the Swiss and French consortiums lost at the USPTO and in district court ([Interprofession du Gruyère v. U.S. Dairy Export Council, No. 22-1041 (4th Cir. 2023), Stanford summary](https://fairuse.stanford.edu/case/interprofession-du-gruyere-v-u-s-dairy-export-council); [Ladas & Parry, May 2, 2023](https://ladas.com/?p=11750) (the FDA description is "silent regarding any geographical source"); [Food Ingredients First, Mar. 6, 2023](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/news/us-dairy-sector-celebrates-ruling-on-gruyere-as-generic-cheese-term.html) (gruyere-style imports 2010–2020 from Switzerland, France, the Netherlands, Germany, Egypt, Denmark, Austria, Belgium, Ireland, the Czech Republic, Italy and Tunisia)). So the chain runs: the best Swiss Gruyère is officially "blind"; in America, "gruyere" must have holes by law, and anyone may make it. Hedges: the AOP text doesn't ban holes outright ("desirable but not indispensable"), so say "few or none," and "the top grade is for rare holes or none"; say "a US appeals court," not "the Supreme Court"; don't say the US "stole" the name. Groove: the 2023 ruling got a news cycle (NPR, Guardian), but the holes-by-law twist and the "aveugle" grade are untold. Risk: "Why does Gruyère have no holes?" is a smaller question than 07's; it works best as a short, dry follow-up. Before voicing, read the Fourth Circuit opinion itself (I used summaries) and the AOP Art. 41 wording in the PDF.
