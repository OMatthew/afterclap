# Short 05 research: "Why can a ripe orange be green?" (and why some were dyed orange)

**Status:** Deep-verified 2026-10-07. Research only, no script yet. Not in the story bank; proposed by the lead as the link out of Short 04.
**Verdict on the working hooks:** keep "Why can a ripe orange be green?" as the opening question and let "why were oranges dyed orange?" be the turn, not the hook. "Are oranges dyed?" is a well-worn listicle fact (Taste of Home, Tasting Table, Legal Planet), and its popular version is now wrong on two counts: the dyeing has stopped (the FDA says no batch of the dye has been certified since 2020), and the "which states ban it" claim is garbled everywhere. The uncovered far end is the law: a Supreme Court fight over dyed oranges (1958) ran straight into the Color Additive Amendments of 1960, and the cancer clause in that law is the exact legal basis for the Red 3 ban that ends Short 04. The orange dye itself was never banned. It was quietly abandoned, and in July 2026 the FDA proposed striking it from the books for that reason.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

A ripe orange is often green. The peel's chlorophyll only breaks down when nights turn cool (UF/IFAS gives about 55 °F), so early-season Florida fruit is sweet inside and green outside, oranges in the tropics stay green, and Valencias left on the tree into a warm spring turn green again ("regreening"). Since 1921, Florida packing houses have "degreened" fruit in gas rooms. The rooms were first heated with kerosene stoves; in 1912 researchers showed the heat wasn't doing it, and in the early 1920s Frank Denny showed the active ingredient was ethylene in the fumes, a natural plant hormone. But gassing only gets Florida's early fruit to pale yellow, so in the 1930s Florida started dyeing the peel too, and stamping the fruit "Color Added". The dye, FD&C Red No. 32, was certified "harmless" in 1939. After the 1950 Halloween candy scare (a different dye, Orange No. 1), the FDA re-tested its colors; Red 32 killed rats within days at 2% of their diet and sickened dogs at 0.01%, and in November 1955 the government struck it off the FDA's list. Florida's growers said the trace on a peel can't hurt anyone (the government conceded there was no evidence it did), and sued. Congress gave them a stopgap law in July 1956. Then, on December 15, 1958, a unanimous Supreme Court (Flemming v. Florida Citrus Exchange) said the 1938 law allowed no such thing as "a little": "where a coal-tar color is not harmless, it is not to be certified; if it is not certified, it is not to be used at all." Congress answered twice. First a second stopgap, Public Law 86-2 of March 17, 1959, which let a new dye, Citrus Red No. 2, onto orange skins "pending congressional consideration of general legislation for the listing and certification of food color additives under safe tolerances". Then that general legislation: the Color Additive Amendments of July 12, 1960, written "to authorize the use of suitable color additives... in accordance with regulations prescribing the conditions (including maximum tolerances) under which such additives may be safely used". The same Act carries a cancer clause: a color that induces cancer in man or animal cannot be listed for ingestion. That clause is the one the FDA invoked in January 2025 to revoke Red 3 (the cherry dye) from food as of January 15, 2027. The orange dye met a different end. The FDA's certification ledgers show the last batches in spring 2020, about 5,500 pounds, and nothing since. On July 23, 2026, the FDA proposed to revoke Citrus Red No. 2 because its use "appears to have been abandoned". **The fruit that forced the law never needed the law's teeth.**

**Recommended chain**
1. A ripe orange can be green. The peel's chlorophyll only breaks down when nights turn cool; warm nights keep it, tropical oranges stay green, and Valencias regreen in spring.
2. Florida has gassed its fruit since 1921, first with kerosene stove fumes, then with ethylene once Denny (1923/24) showed that was the active part.
3. Gas gets Florida's early fruit to pale yellow, not orange. So in the 1930s Florida also dyed the peel and stamped the fruit "Color Added". Red 32 was certified harmless in 1939.
4. 1950 Halloween candy (Orange No. 1) made children sick, so the FDA re-tested its colors. Red 32 failed in rats and dogs. November 1955: struck off.
5. Florida: it's a trace on a peel, and nobody has been hurt (the government conceded that). Congress: fine, three more years (July 1956).
6. ★ Dec. 15, 1958, Supreme Court, unanimous: the 1938 law has no room for tolerances. Not harmless, not certified, not used at all.
7. ★ March 17, 1959: Public Law 86-2 lets a new dye, Citrus Red No. 2, onto orange skins at 2 ppm "pending... general legislation... under safe tolerances". July 12, 1960: that legislation, the Color Additive Amendments, with its cancer clause.
8. Far end: the cancer clause is what is banning the cherry's Red 3 from January 2027. The orange's dye was never banned: no batch since 2020, and in July 2026 the FDA proposed to strike it as abandoned.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"A ripe orange can be green"** and **"cool nights"**, not a hard temperature. If a number is used: "about 55 degrees".
- **"The gas doesn't ripen it; it only changes the peel."** Fruit must already meet maturity standards.
- **"In the early 1920s"** for Denny (sources say 1923 and 1924), **"1921"** for Florida's first kerosene coloring rooms, **"in the 1930s"** for the start of dyeing.
- **"Some Florida and Texas oranges, early in the season, for the fresh market"**, never "oranges are dyed". Juice oranges were excluded by the rule itself.
- **"A different orange dye, in Halloween candy"** for 1950. Red 32 failed the re-tests that followed; it wasn't the candy dye.
- **"The Court said the law allowed no tolerances"**, not "the Supreme Court banned the dye". The Court upheld the FDA's delisting; Congress then allowed a new dye.
- **"Part of the reason Congress rewrote the color law in 1960"**, never "all because of oranges". The 1959 orange law's own title says "pending... general legislation"; that's the honest strength of the link.
- **"The same clause"**: the 2025 Red 3 order cites the 1960 cancer clause (now section 721(b)(5)(B)). Don't say the 1960 law "banned Red 3"; the FDA did, in 2025, under it.
- **"No batch has been certified since 2020"** (the FDA's wording is "appears"); **"the FDA has proposed to strike it"**. If a final order has issued by upload day, say "struck it... as abandoned".
- **No health claims about Citrus Red No. 2, Red 3 or Red 40.** Not IARC, not "carcinogen", not "safe". The 2026 proposal is about abandonment, not safety. Red 3's ban is legal (Delaney), not proof of human harm (04-maraschino.md).
- **"Color Added"** is what Florida and Texas law require on boxes/fruit; the FDA allows a counter sign for loose fruit. Don't claim every dyed orange was stamped.
- **Dates:** Dec. 15, 1958 (decision); July 9, 1956 and March 17, 1959 (laws); July 12, 1960 (Act); Jan. 15, 2027 (Red 3 food); July 23, 2026 (proposal).

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Most oranges are dyed."** Only some early-season Florida/Texas fresh-market oranges ever were, and the FDA's ledgers show the last batch in 2020. Florida repealed its coloring procedures in 2018 as "no longer utilized by the fresh industry". Tasting Table's "for decades, citrus growers have dyed their produce" is stale.
2. **"California and Arizona (or Texas) ban it."** Texas law provides for it with "Color Added" marking ([Agric. Code 95.017](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/AG/htm/AG.95.htm)); California exempts out-of-state oranges stamped "color added" ([3 CCR 1365.3](https://regulations.justia.com/states/california/title-3/division-3/chapter-1/subchapter-4/article-1/section-1365-3)); Arizona: no statute found. [Legal Planet](https://legal-planet.org/2025/01/16/red-3-is-going/) says "California and Texas prohibit"; [Tasting Table](https://tastingtable.com/1259716/determine-orange-peels-artifically-dyed) says "California or Arizona". Leave states out, or say only "Florida and Texas allowed it, stamped Color Added".
3. **"Citrus Red 2 is being banned as a carcinogen."** The July 2026 proposal says abandoned, outdated, unnecessary. (IARC 2B exists; the FDA said in 1978 it was safe as used. Stay out of it.)
4. **"Citrus Red 2 was approved in 1956."** Wikipedia says so; the primary record says temporary listing April 17, 1959 under PL 86-2 (March 17, 1959), permanent May 22, 1963. 1956 was the Red 32 stopgap.
5. **"The dye soaks into the fruit."** It stays on the peel (Hall 2013), and processing oranges were excluded by the rule.
6. **"A green orange is unripe" / "the gas ripens it."** Peel color and internal maturity are separate in warm climates; ethylene changes the peel of fruit that already passed maturity tests.
7. **"The Halloween candy was dyed with the orange dye."** The candy had Orange No. 1. Red 32 was caught in the re-testing that followed.
8. **"The word orange comes from the fruit."** True but groove; out.
9. **"Congress exempted oranges from the cancer clause."** I found no orange exception in the 1960 Act. Its transitional section carried Citrus Red No. 2 over as a provisionally listed color like ~200 others; the FDA listed it permanently in 1963.

## Bridge from Short 04

**Short 04's planned last beat:** one 1910 "maraschino" sample was dyed with erythrosine, now FD&C Red No. 3, approved 1907, banned from US food from January 15, 2027 (most maraschinos now use Red 40; Red 3 lingers in fruit-cocktail cherries).

**Is there a real shared thing?** Yes, and it's the strongest bridge so far: a shared rule, a shared agency and a shared idea, plus a shared object.
- **Real, the rule:** Red 3 is being revoked under the cancer clause of the Color Additive Amendments of 1960 ([FDA order](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-16/html/2025-00830.htm)). That Act is the "general legislation... under safe tolerances" that the 1959 orange law was written to wait for ([73 Stat. 3](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-73/pdf/STATUTE-73-Pg3.pdf)), after the Supreme Court's orange ruling said the old law allowed no tolerances. So Short 05 can honestly say the clause that ends the cherry's dye sits in a law that oranges helped force into being. Hedge: "part of the reason".
- **Real, the idea:** dye applied so food looks the way we expect, and the rules that grew up around it. 04: a bleached cherry dyed red, then "dyed red" written into the definition. 05: a green-ripe orange gassed and dyed orange, stamped "Color Added", and a law written to allow "a little".
- **Real, the object:** both are bar fruit. The orange slice and cherry on a cocktail pick is a classic garnish, and the Short 04 last scene can show a cherry beside an orange slice. Also real: both dyes were FDA-certified coal-tar colors with numbers (Red 3; Red 32 then Citrus Red 2), and both carried a government stamp of what they are ("dyed red"; "Color Added").
- **Not real, don't imply:** that Citrus Red No. 2 is Red 3, or chemically related to it (it isn't; it's an azo dye); that oranges and cherries were dyed by the same people; that the orange dye was banned. The 1960 Act had several drivers; oranges are one, well documented.
- **Write backward (plant the orange in 04):** show an orange slice with the cherry in the cocktail garnish scene, or, if 04 mentions the 1907 list of seven dyes, note that one of them was an orange dye (Orange No. 1) that made children sick on Halloween 1950, but only after verifying the 1907 list from the decision itself (single-sourced above).
- **Suggested last line for Short 04 (choose one; the thread lives only in the last line):**
  1. "And why would anyone dye an orange? That's another story." (Safest. On screen: the cherry jar, and beside it an orange slice on a cocktail pick, the paint sliding from cherry to peel.)
  2. "And the law that's retiring the cherry's red was shaped by a fight over oranges. Why would anyone dye an orange? That's another story." (True as stated; longer.)
  3. "And why can a ripe orange be green? That's another story." (Matches 05's hook exactly, but the shared object on screen must then be the orange itself, which is weaker as a tease from a cherry.)

**Possible bridges out of 05 (leads only, to keep two ahead):** Hawaii statehood the next day (PL 86-3), ethylene and why bananas are gassed (a known story, groove risk), the 1959 cranberry scare (the same Secretary Flemming, that November), Orange B and hot-dog casings (revoked Sept. 2026), or kerosene lamps.
