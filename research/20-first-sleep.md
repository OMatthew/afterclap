Claims to check (two sources each; Ekirch's own work first):

- Doctors' advice on which side to lie on for each half: verify.
- Faded with artificial light, late hours and industrial work; "by about the 1920s" the term had gone: Ekirch's dating.
- Thomas Wehr (NIMH), 1992: volunteers given 14 hours of darkness a night for about a month began sleeping in two bouts with a quiet waking between. Check the paper (J Sleep Res 1992) for exact setup.
Present-day angle (why it matters now): waking at 3am is often treated as insomnia; sleep researchers on whether "middle-of-the-night waking" is normal; screens and light. Don't give medical advice; one careful line at most.

### Corrections to the X post
1. **1697 is wrong (or at least unsupported): the published date is 13 April 1699.** Jane Rowth was nine.
2. "Nobody slept through the night": no. Common in pre-industrial Europe by Ekirch's evidence; Yetish 2015 found no such pattern in three groups living without electricity near the equator, and Boyce 2023 disputes how far the texts go.
3. "A dozen languages": one source (CNN). "About two thousand references, back to Homer" is Ekirch's own.
4. "By the 1920s": a journalist's line. Ekirch dates the change to the 1800s, complete by the early 1900s.
5. Wehr: don't give the head count (sources say 7, 8, 15 and 16). It was about three to four weeks, and the volunteers were kept in a dark room, not just "given darkness"; critics note that is unlike a pre-industrial night with fire, candles and bedfellows.
6. "Medieval" is the usual label, but the bulk of Ekirch's evidence is early modern (1500s-1700s), with references back to Homer. "For centuries" is safer than "in the Middle Ages".
7. What people did in the gap (prayed, talked, tended the fire, sex, visited neighbours, stole) is Ekirch's (BBC 2022, Virginia Tech 2017): fine to use, lightly.
