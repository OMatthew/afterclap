# Short 14 research: "Why do we walk through haunted houses on Halloween?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-27 for a post Oct 30-31, 2026. Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** "Where did haunted houses come from?" Seasonal and easy to hook. Neutral (no politics, no brands).
**Verdict:** **Go, but tell it against the usual answer, and do not use the lead as written.** The usual answer (Smithsonian 2015, History.com 2018, Wikipedia: Halloween haunted houses "seemed to begin emerging during the Great Depression," as a way to keep teens from pranking) is **half right**. The pranking is real and so is the push to give kids something else to do. But **the haunted house is not a Depression invention.** I found U.S. newspapers describing a "haunted house" Halloween party in **1912** (Chicago), a church-youth "Haunted House" in a manse in **1915** (Hurley, South Dakota: guests sent two at a time "up the stairway that leads to death," a ghost at the top), and the "feel the dead man's brains (a damp sponge) and eyes (peeled grapes) in the dark" routine in a California paper in **1924**. A children's magazine had a dark-basement ghost scene in **1905**. What the Depression changed was **who ran it**: towns, Boy Scouts and Junior Chambers of Commerce started throwing organized Halloween parties because pranks had turned ugly (Rhinelander, Wisc., 1931; Fargo, N.D., 1934, "Halloween Damage Light"). Charities then found people would pay for a walk-through (Indianapolis Children's Museum Guild, 1964, first year over $13,000 at 50 cents; Jaycees chapters from 1970), and Disney turned a planned guided walk-through into a ride (1969). **Not verified:** that "parents' groups, schools and magazines" promoted *haunted houses specifically* as the anti-prank tool. That rests on one author (Lisa Morton) and one 1937 pamphlet I could not identify. Madame Tussaud (1802) and the 1915 Orton and Spooner ghost house are a different lineage (paid wax museum, fairground ride), not the Halloween one; treat as side notes or skip.

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

The tidy story says haunted houses began in the 1930s. Lisa Morton (author of *Trick or Treat: A History of Halloween*, 2012) says in Smithsonian that they "came in about the same time as trick-or-treat did," as parents tried to curb escalating pranks, and cites "a 1937 party pamphlet" that tells a host to build a "trail of terror" in the cellar or attic. That pamphlet exists in the sense that History.com and Smithsonian both quote it, but neither names its title or publisher and I could not find it.

The older papers say the *idea* was already there. In October 1912 a Chicago daily ran a party column that says "the fortune-telling feature of the 'haunted house' party must not be forgotten," so a "haunted house party" was a known type of home party. In November 1915 a South Dakota weekly reported that a Presbyterian young people's class held "A Haunted House" at the manse: groans from the yard, a ghost at the door, guests led "two at a time" up "the stairway that leads to death," and "at the top a ghost pounced upon them." In October 1924 a Torrance, California, paper printed a Halloween program in which a "ghost" passes a sponge "dampened with ice water" as the victim's "brains" and "two grapes from which the skins have been removed" as his eyes, to guests in the dark. That is the same trick as the "damp sponges" and "hair nets" in the 1937 pamphlet, 13 years earlier. So the haunted house began as a **living-room party game**, not a Depression idea.

What the early 1930s did change is the scale. Halloween vandalism was a real, worsening problem. The Fargo *Forum* called Oct 31, 1933 "the roughest Halloween night in recent years." In Rhinelander, Wisconsin, the Boy Scouts and city council threw a town Halloween party in 1931 and "police reported that vandalism dropped considerably" (it was not repeated in 1932, and the pranks came back). In Fargo, local businessman Fred Maage proposed "a night of pleasant entertainment with nothing 'sissy' about it," the Junior Chamber of Commerce (Jaycees) ran two parties in 1934, and the *Forum* headlined "Halloween Damage Light as Youths of City Cavort at Two Celebrations." A 1937 Fargo party was explicitly aimed at "4,000 potential Halloween pranksters." Morton says neighbors "pooled their resources and staged house-to-house parties," one house giving out costumes, the next treats, another "a basement set up as a tiny haunt." That is the real Depression link: a home scare got folded into a community fix for vandalism.

The next step is the money. In 1964 a volunteer women's group, the Indianapolis Children's Museum Guild (founded 1933), turned an old building into "The Haunted House," on an idea from a member who had moved there; first year, over $13,000 from 50-cent tickets, and it still runs. In 1970 the Sycamore-Deer Park Jaycees and a Cincinnati radio station, WSAI, opened a 16-room, $1 haunted house; Cincinnati's local scene says it "got its start" there. The Jaycees spread charity haunted houses across the U.S. (Cedar Rapids 1971, among many). **A neat loop:** the same kind of group, a Jaycees chapter, ran Fargo's anti-prank *parties* in 1934, and a Fargo account says the Jaycees later added "haunted houses, parades and community fundraisers." (We have no proof the 1970s haunts descended from the 1930s parties. They are a pattern, not a lineage.) Disneyland's Haunted Mansion opened Aug 9, 1969; Walt asked Ken Anderson in early 1957 to design it, and the first plan was a **walk-through with a guide leading groups of 40**, which was dropped for a ride (the "doom buggies").

**Recommended chain** (six beats, ~65 s; beat 6 is the cut if long; one number per beat at most)
1. ★ **Hook, groove broken in the first line.** "Haunted houses started in the Depression to keep kids from pranking. That's the story. Newspapers say the idea is older." Hedge: "the usual story."
2. ★ **Older than the story.** 1915, a small South Dakota church: the young people's class turns the manse into "A Haunted House." Guests go up the stairs two at a time. A ghost jumps out at the top. (Option: the 1924 "brains" sponge and peeled-grape "eyes" as a visual gag.)
3. ★ **The 1930s did change something.** Pranks got rough. The Fargo paper called 1933 "the roughest Halloween night in recent years." So towns threw parties instead. In 1934 Fargo's Junior Chamber of Commerce ran two; the paper's headline the next day: "Halloween Damage Light."
4. ★ **The party goes house to house.** "Historians say" neighbors pooled what they had: one house gave costumes, one gave treats, one a basement set up as "a tiny haunt" (Morton). Attributed. This is the only place the "haunt as prank-fix" idea appears; keep it short and hedged.
5. ★ **Charity finds the money.** 1964, Indianapolis: a women's volunteer guild turned an old building into a haunted house for 50 cents a ticket, raised over $13,000 in the first year. Then Jaycees chapters copied it across the country (Cincinnati, 1970).
6. **Disney, last and small.** "Disney's was planned as a guided walk-through, like a party tour. It opened in 1969 as a ride." End: "A scare in a church basement turned into a business." No "Happy Halloween" and no moral.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"The usual story" / "historians say."** The Depression link is Morton's account, not a document I could read. Don't say "parents' groups and magazines promoted haunted houses to stop pranks."
- **"A South Dakota paper from 1915."** The 1912, 1915 and 1924 items are OCR reads; the idea might be older still, so say "at least as far back as" not "the first."
- **The anti-prank parties are real; haunted houses weren't their stated tool.** Fargo's and Rhinelander's parties were movies, dances, wrestling and a parade, not haunted houses. Only Morton's "tiny haunt" ties the two. Keep beat 4 short and attributed.
- **Numbers:** "over $13,000" (not "$13,000"), "50 cents." 1964 is the Indianapolis museum's start, not the first charity haunt in the country (Wikipedia mentions 1962-63 home haunts, unsourced).
- **Jaycees:** "a Jaycees chapter in Cincinnati," not "the Jaycees invented the haunted house." Don't imply the 1934 Fargo parties led to the 1970 haunts.
- **Disney:** "Walt asked for it in 1957; the first plan was a guided walk-through; it opened in 1969." Not "Walt invented the haunted house."

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Haunted houses started in the Great Depression."** Smithsonian, History.com and Wikipedia say so (all from Morton). Newspapers of 1912, 1915 and 1924 describe the same thing.
2. **"Haunted houses were invented to stop Halloween pranks."** No primary source ties haunted houses (as opposed to parties) to anti-vandalism. The anti-prank parties are real.
3. **"The 1915 Orton and Spooner ghost train was the first haunted attraction."** It's a "ghost house" the owner dates "around 1915." Orton and Spooner's own archive says they started ghost *trains* in the 1930s. "First to charge admission" is an unsourced Wikipedia line, and Tussaud charged for her Chamber of Horrors a century earlier.
4. **"Madame Tussaud's Chamber of Horrors is the first haunted house."** A wax museum, not a Halloween or walk-in-the-dark thing. The 1802 date is her London arrival; the "Chamber of Horrors" name came in the 1840s.
5. **"Disney invented the haunted house."** First planned 1951-57, opened 1969, after church basements, charities and Jaycees chapters (the first planned walk-through even looks like the church manse).
6. **"Black Halloween, 1933" with flipped cars and sawed poles.** Unsourced in History.com.
7. **"Kawash and Rogers explain haunted houses."** Kawash is about trick-or-treat; Rogers's popular summaries don't cover this.
8. **"Hell houses started with the Jaycees."** Smithsonian puts the first evangelical "hell houses" at 1972 (Falwell). Off topic; don't mention.

## Groove risk
- **What generic AI Shorts say:** "Haunted houses began with Madame Tussaud's wax figures in 1802, then the Orton and Spooner ghost train in 1915, then Disney in 1969." Smooth, eye-rolling, and (as above) partly wrong. Also "during the Great Depression, parents invented haunted houses to keep kids out of trouble."
- **How we differ:** open by saying the Depression story is half right, then show a South Dakota church manse in 1915 and a damp sponge for brains in 1924, then the real 1930s change (towns throwing parties, Fargo's "Damage Light"), then the money (Indianapolis, 50 cents). The audience gets a correction, an honest hedge, and a small human picture, not a timeline of famous names.
- **Visual groove to avoid:** haunted mansion silhouettes, bats and full moons, jack-o'-lantern clip art, Halloween orange, spooky fonts, cobwebs. Keep it dry and a little funny, like a church social.
