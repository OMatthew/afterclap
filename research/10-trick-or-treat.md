# Short 10 research: "Why do we say 'trick or treat'?"

**Status:** Researched 2026-10-15 for a post around Oct 21. Research only, no script yet.
**Candidate:** the link out of 09, as written in 09's narration ("And why do we say 'trick or treat'? That's another story."). Found in the topic research of 2026-10-14 (row 4, "why do we say" → trick or treat).
**Verdict:** safe to tease as written, and the true story beats the usual one. **The "trick" was a real threat, and the "treat" was protection money.** The earliest known printed use is not Alberta in 1927. It's Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario, Nov. 1, 1917, and it describes a protection racket: "'Tricks or treats' you could hear the gangs call out, and if the householder passed out the 'coin' for the 'treats' his establishment would be immune from attack until another gang came along that knew not of or had no part in the agreement." Through the 1930s, US papers wrote it up as a gangster shakedown ("employing modern shake-down methods," Oregon 1934; "In return the youngsters offered protection against window soaping," Reno 1938). Montana's 1934 story names the ringleader "Pretty Boy John Doe," two weeks after the FBI killed Pretty Boy Floyd. Three corrections to the brief: (1) **Adults didn't invent the treat as a bribe.** Towns did throw parties and parades to head off pranks (Anoka, 1920), but the one historian who went through the newspapers finds "no evidence that they suggested or initiated" trick-or-treating; kids did (Kawash 2011). (2) **WWII didn't stop it.** Sugar was rationed from 1942 to 1947 and some towns discouraged door-to-door begging, but "beggar's night" went on. Say "made treats scarce," not "paused." (3) **There's no proven direct line from souling or guising.** The customs look alike, but scholars find "no direct line" and call the claim of direct descent "mere fiction" (Kawash, citing Tuleja). **Recommended far end:** the threat flipped. By the 1970s parents feared the trick would come *from* the house: poison, razor blades. Sociologist Joel Best has found no substantiated case of a child killed or seriously hurt by a treat from a stranger, and the famous 1974 death was a father poisoning his own son. But homemade treats were finished. Candy historian Samira Kawash: if it "wasn't sealed in a recognizable, major brand factory label, then it was guilty until proven innocent." **Bridge from 09: the wrapper holds as a shared object and a shared idea, but not as a cause** (see Bridge verdict).

## Summary: the safest, most surprising chain

For most of its history, Halloween night in North America meant pranks, not candy: gates carried off, wagons on roofs, outhouses tipped over, doorbells rung for fun. Towns fought back with curfews, parties and parades. Anoka, Minnesota, started its civic Halloween in 1920 so kids would be "having so much fun that they wouldn't do the pranks." Then a new move appears at the door. The earliest known printed "tricks or treats" is from Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario, on Nov. 1, 1917, and it already describes a deal: pay the gang a coin and your house is "immune from attack until another gang came along that knew not of or had no part in the agreement." The phrase spread across the Canadian prairies in the 1920s ("Treat up, or tricks," Edmonton 1922; "trick or treat," Lethbridge 1927, the citation most articles still call the first) and into the US West in the 1930s. US newspapers framed it the way the 1930s framed everything: as gangsters. "Modern shake-down methods" (Oregon, 1934). "Pretty Boy John Doe," "the Gangsters of Tomorrow" (Montana, 1934, two weeks after Pretty Boy Floyd was killed). "In return the youngsters offered protection against window soaping" (Reno, 1938). The Los Angeles Times (1938): "the diminutive Halloween goon squads are bought off with cookies, candy, tickless alarm clocks or the price of an ice cream cone." That's what the word "treat" meant: the payment that bought off the trick. Historian Samira Kawash argues the kids invented it, using the gangster story they saw everywhere: souling asked for charity; trick-or-treating "more closely resembled extortion." The war made sugar scarce (rationed from spring 1942 to June 1947). After the war, pranks faded, magazines and radio shows normalized the custom (American Home and Jack and Jill in 1947, Jack Benny in 1948), families began buying treats instead of making them, and by the 1960s candy makers were selling mini bars sized for the door. Then the threat turned around. In 1959 a California dentist was accused of handing out laxatives as Halloween candy; through the 1960s and 70s came stories of razor blades and poison. Joel Best has tracked these reports since 1958 and has found no substantiated case of a child killed or seriously hurt by a contaminated treat from trick-or-treating. The best-known death, Timothy O'Bryan in Pasadena, Texas, in 1974, was murder by his own father. But the stories won. Homemade popcorn balls and apples became suspect, and the factory wrapper became the proof of safety. So "trick or treat" began as a kid's threat to the house, and ended with the house's treat being the thing parents feared.

**Recommended chain** (six beats, ~60 s; beat 4 is the one to cut if long)
1. **The question, and a real answer:** the trick was a real threat. Before candy, Halloween night meant pranks: gates gone, wagons on roofs. (One line, drawn.)
2. ★ **The first time it's printed, it's a protection deal.** Ontario, 1917: "Tricks or treats," the gangs called out. Pay them a coin and your house was safe, the paper said, until a gang that didn't know about the deal came along. (Paraphrase; quote "immune from attack" if it reads well.)
3. ★ **Newspapers in the 1930s wrote it up like a mob shakedown.** Kids "offered protection against window soaping" (Reno, 1938); in Montana the leader was nicknamed after Pretty Boy Floyd, killed two weeks earlier. The treat was literally what you paid so nothing happened. (Hedge: "papers described it as.")
4. *(Optional)* **The war made sugar scarce; afterwards it went national.** Pranks faded; families started buying treats; candy makers made bars small enough to hand out.
5. ★ **Then the trick turned around.** In the 1970s, parents were warned that the person at the door might hurt the kids: poison, razor blades. A sociologist who has tracked these stories since 1958 can't find a single stranger who did it. The famous 1974 death was the boy's own father.
6. ★ **Last beat: the wrapper.** Homemade treats were out. If it wasn't sealed in a factory wrapper, it was suspect. "So the treat that bought off the trick now comes wrapped against a trick that, as far as anyone can find, strangers never played." Then the tease.

## Hedges the narration must use
- **"The earliest known printed use"** (1917, Ontario). Not "invented in 1917" or "invented in Canada." The practice may be older than the print.
- **"Tricks or treats"**: the 1917 paper has the plural. Fine to say "trick or treat" in the line and show the plural only if the drawing has text (it shouldn't).
- **Protection deal:** quote or close-paraphrase the paper ("pay the coin and the house was safe until another gang came along"). It's one paper's description, so "a local paper described it as."
- **"Newspapers wrote it up like a gangster shakedown"**, not "kids were gangsters." The papers chose the words; Kawash argues kids played the role.
- **Adults vs kids:** "the kids demanded and the grown-ups paid." Never "towns invented trick-or-treating to bribe kids" (disputed).
- **WWII:** "sugar was rationed" / "treats were scarce." Never "trick-or-treating stopped" or "paused."
- **Poison:** "no one has found a single case of a stranger killing a child with Halloween candy" (Best: no substantiated report of a child killed or seriously injured). The 1974 case: "the boy's own father." Don't say "never happened," and don't name the father or the child on screen.
- **Wrapper:** "the factory wrapper became the proof it was safe." Don't say the scare *invented* wrapped mini candy (mini bars: 1961; "fun size": 1968, before the 1970s scares).
- **Candy makers:** "joined in later." Not "candy companies invented Halloween/trick-or-treating," and not "spread the poison myth."
- **Older customs:** "kids had gone door to door before, singing for treats. The threat was new." Not "trick-or-treating comes from souling/Samhain."
- **No "chain"** in the narration (the paint carries it).

## Myths to avoid
1. **"Trick-or-treating comes from Samhain / Celtic spirits / souling."** The customs look alike, but the documented path has gaps; scholars call direct descent "mere fiction" (Tuleja via Kawash). This is the main groove.
2. **"First printed in Alberta, 1927."** That was the record until the 1917 Sault Ste. Marie find (Popik; DCHP-3). National Geographic still gives "a 1927 Alberta newspaper article"; Smithsonian's piece says the early 1920s.
3. **"Adults invented it to bribe pranksters."** Disputed. Adults did use treats and parties against pranks; the door demand came from kids (Kawash).
4. **"It stopped during WWII."** Sugar was rationed 1942–47; beggar's night went on in places.
5. **"Candy companies invented trick-or-treating."** Candy trade journals of the 1920s–30s don't even mention Halloween; marketing came in the 1950s.
6. **"Strangers poison Halloween candy."** No substantiated case of a child killed or seriously hurt (Best). The 1974 case was the father; the 1970 Detroit case was heroin from a relative's home.
7. **"Wrapped mini candy was invented because of the poison scare."** Mini bars from 1961, "fun size" 1968; the scare made wrapped candy the only acceptable treat.
8. **"Trick-or-treat was always for candy."** In the 1930s: nuts, coins, toys, cookies, popcorn balls, fruit.
9. **"'Trick or treat' is an American phrase."** Every citation before 1928 is Canadian (DCHP labels it a Canadianism).

## Bridge verdict (from 09)

**09's last scene (narration):** "So the slice stayed the same. Only the wrapper changed." Then: "And why do we say 'trick or treat'? That's another story."

**Does the wrapper bridge hold?**
- **Real, the object: the individual wrapper.** 09 ends on the plastic-wrapped slice. 10 ends on the factory-wrapped candy. Both are single servings sealed at the factory, from the same era (wrapped slices 1956/1965; mini bars 1961, "fun size" 1968). Showing the wrapped slice beside a wrapped candy in 09's last frame is honest.
- **Real, the idea: trusting the wrapper, not what's inside.** In 09, the words on the wrapper changed and the food didn't. In 10, the factory wrapper became the proof a treat was safe, guarding against a danger nobody has been able to document. Both are about what a wrapper promises.
- **Not real (don't claim it):** that individually wrapped cheese slices led to individually wrapped candy, or that the poisoning scares created the wrapped candy bar. No source links the two, and mini bars came before the 1970s scares. Kraft Singles' wrapping was about convenience and keeping, not poison.
